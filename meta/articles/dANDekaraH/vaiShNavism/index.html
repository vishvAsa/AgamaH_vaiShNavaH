RGB’s survey and origin theory

RGB’s survey of Vaiṣṇavism from about the fifth century before Christ to the middle of the seventeenth century after Christ may be broadly divided into three main parts. In the first part he explains how that religious sect originated and how it passed through various stages of development before it finally came to be consolidated in the form of what may be called classical Vaiṣṇavism. While this first part is historical in character, the emphasis in the second part is more on the doctrine. In the eighth century A. D., Śaṃkarācārya vigorously promulgated ’the doctrine of spiritual monism and world-illusion’—a doctrine which was considered to be highly detrimental to the basic tenets of Vaiṣṇavism. It had to be confronted and refuted on a logical-metaphysical level and the religion of Bhakti had to be rehabilitated on sound rational basis. This is exactly what Rāmānuja (11th century) strenuously endeavoured to do. The same mission was carried forward, in their own distinctive ways, by Nimbārka (12th century), Madhva (13th century), and Vallabha (15th century). The second part of RGB’s survey deals with the thought and work of these Ācāryas. An active sympathy for the lower classes and the use of vernaculars for the propagation of Bhakti with special stress on its emotional side had become evident in the history of Vaiṣṇavism ever since the days of Rāmānuja. The compositions of the Āḻvārs of the south would confirm this. But these traits became more marked with Rāmānanda (born 1300 A. D.). They continued to be the hallmark of the teachings and activities of the later Vaiṣṇava saints like Kabir (1398–1518) and Tulasīdāsa (1532–1623) in the north, Caitanya (1485–1533) in the east, and Nāmadeva (born 1270?) and Tukārāma (1607–1649) in the Deccan. The discussion of these teachings and activities may be said to constitute the third part of RGB’s survey of Vaiṣṇavism. Out of these three parts it is the first which is particularly important from the historical point of view, the other two parts being more or less of the nature of factual statement. The first part is also more controversial and calls for a critical re-appraisal, in the light of the fresh evidence that has become available, of some of the [[doints|points]] made in it.

[[P233]] About the origin and growth of Vaiṣṇavism, RGB has to say this: “It (Vaiṣṇavism) first appeared as a religious reform, like Buddhism and Jainism, but based on theistic principles. Its early name was Ekāntika Dharma, or the religion of a single-minded love and devotion to One. In its background stood the Bhagavad-gītā, a discourse professing to be preached by Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa. It soon assumed a sectarian form and was called the Pāñcarātra or Bhāgavata religion. It was professed by a tribe of Kṣatriyas of the name of Sātvata and was noticed by Megasthenes, about the end of the fourth century before Christ, as the religion of such a specific people. This faith mingled itself with the existing one in Nārāyaṇa… and with that in Viṣṇu. In the Bhagavad-gītā itself were embodied some of the teachings of the Upaniṣads…. Soon after the [[bringing|beginning]] of the Christian era another element was contributed to this system of religion by the Ābhīras, or cowherds, who belonged to a foreign tribe, in the shape of the marvellous deeds of the boy-Kṛṣṇa, who came to be regarded as a god, and of his amorous dalliances with cowherdesses. So constituted, Vaiṣṇavism went on till about the end of the eighth century….”1

Pre-Vedic Bhakti and Muni-Yati cults

When RGB says that Vaiṣṇavism first appeared as a religious reform, he seems to suggest that it was orthodox in origin and that it represented an extension of Vedic thought in a particular direction. According to him, the tide of free speculations initiated by the Upaniṣads culminated in the east into such systems as Buddhism and Jainism and in the west into a theistic system with a god who had come to dwell among them. The tendency to trace all religious ideologies—indeed, the entire Indian culture—back to the Veda, in some way or the other, which characterised early Indology, is evident here. It must, however, be remembered that when the Vedic Aryans arrived in India they did not by any means step into a religious vacuum. There is sufficient evidence to show that different religious sects prevailed in different regions of the pre-Vedic non-Aryan India. It is possible to identify at least two such pre-Vedic non-Aryan cults. One may designate [[P234]] them as the Muni-Yati-cult and the Bhakti-cult with a view to distinguishing them from the exotic Vedic Aryan cult which may be called the Ṛṣi-cult. The Muni-Yati-cult, with its emphasis on Yoga, Tapas, Saṃnyāsa, etc., was a characteristic feature of the pre-Vedic non-Aryan Śiva-religion of the Indus valley as also of the ancient Magadhan religious complex which served as the fountainhead of Jainism and Buddhism,2 while the autochthonous character of Bhakti, as evidenced, among others, by its connections with the indigenous worship of the Mother-Goddess and the Nāga- and the Yakṣa-cults, its vegetal and fertility symbolism, and the bacchalian element in it, is now generally accepted.3 It can also be seen that the Muni-Yati-cult and the Bhakti-cult did not fail to influence the Ṛṣi-cult, both positively and negatively. The Vedic religion adopted the pre-Vedic non-Aryan Śiva in the form of Rudra,4 while it is not improbable that Vasiṣṭha’s attitude towards Varuṇa reflects the influence of the Bhakti-cult.5 Negatively, the hieratic Vedic religion exhibited obvious loathness for the Yatis, the Śiśna-devas, and the Mūra-devas.6

On account of its freshness, energy, and flush of victory, the Vedic religion succeeded in keeping the indigenous popular religious sects suppressed for a fairly long time. But when, about the end of the period of the major Upaniṣads (7th–6th centuries B. C.), the hold of Vedism, for obvious reasons, began to weaken, those popular religious cults again gathered strength—indeed, to such an extent that they soon posed a serious challenge to the Vedic religion itself.7 There was, however, some difference in the attitudes of the religious sects mentioned above. While Jainism and Buddhism openly revolted against the hegemony of [[P235]] Vedism, the Bhakti-religion, for some practical reasons, sought to establish a kind of liaison with it.

Vāsudevism

Bhakti for a personal god constitutes the very kernel of Vaiṣṇavism. It would seem that the theistic religion, with Bhakti for the deified Vṛṣṇi hero Vāsudeva (who, it may be emphasized, is not mentioned in any early Vedic text) as its central tenet, was one of the several sects which re-emerged as powerful religious forces in the days of the decline of Vedism. Incidentally, it may be noted that, like Vāsudeva who belonged to the tribe of the Vṛṣṇis, Mahāvīra and Gautama Buddha who consolidated their religious doctrines (the origin of which, as pointed out earlier, can be traced back to the pre-Vedic Magadhan thought complex), at about this very time, also belonged to the tribes of the Jñātṛkas and the Śākyas respectively. RGB has rightly indicated that the starting point of what we today understand by Vaiṣṇavism was not the worship of Viṣṇu—it was the worship of Vāsudeva. This Vāsudevism, which represented the primary phase of Vaiṣṇavism, must have become fairly well established in the days of Pāṇini (5th century B. C.), for, Pāṇini was required to enunciate a special Sūtra (IV. 3. 98) governing the formation of the word vāsudevaka in the sense of a ‘Bhakta or devotee of the pre-eminently worshipful god Vāsudeva’.8 The tradition of the supreme godhead of Vāsudeva and of Vāsudevism has been continuous since that time. Megasthenes (4th century B. C.) speaks of the people of Śūrasena who held Heracles (by whom Megasthenes obviously meant Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa) in special veneration.9 As pointed out by RGB, a Niddesa passage also suggests that Vāsudeva-worship prevailed in the 4th century B. C.10 The Bhagavad-gītā (3rd century B. C.) eulogises the man of knowledge, who at the end of several births, betakes himself unto the Lord in the conviction that “Vāsudeva is All” (VII. 19). We

[[P236]] then have the Besnagar inscription, belonging to the last quarter of the second century B. C., on the Garuḍa-dhvaja of Vāsudeva, the god of gods, erected by Heliodoros, the Bhāgavata, the son of Dion and an inhabitant of Takṣaśilā.

The historical tradition that the god Vāsudeva originally belonged to the tribe of the Vṛṣṇis is well attested. For instance, in the Bhagavad-gītā, the Lord unequivocally declares that of the Vṛṣṇis he is Vāsudeva (X. 37). The Mahā-bhāṣya of Patañjali (150 B. C.) also clearly speaks of Vāsudeva as belonging to the Vṛṣṇi tribe.11 Again, as pointed out by RGB, Vāsudeva is closely related to the other deified Vṛṣṇi hero Saṃkarṣaṇa in the Ghosundi stone inscription of Sarvatāta, belonging to the second half of the first century B. C., and the Nanaghat cave inscription of the Sātavāhana queen Nāganikā of the first century B. C.12 The Kāśikā emphasizes this close relationship between Saṃkarṣaṇa and Vāsudeva by giving the example, dvandvaṃ [[saṃkarṣaṇavāsudavau|saṃkarṣaṇavāsudevau]], to illustrate P. VIII. 1. 15, atyantasahacarite loka-vijñāte dvandvam ity upasaṃkhyānam.

Spread and Jaina-Buddhist notices

Though the religion centering round the popular god Vāsudeva had become well established since before Pāṇini’s time, it is strange that the Buddhist canonical text Aṅguttara-nikāya,13 which mentions such religious sects as the Ājīvikas, the Nirgranthas, the Muṇḍa-śrāvakas, etc., does not refer to the Vāsudevakas. Similarly Aśoka’s inscriptions, which mention the Śramaṇas, the Brāhmaṇas, the Ājīvikas, and the Nirgranthas, do not speak of the Vāsudevakas. The only possible explanation of this would be that Vāsudevism, which was naturally restricted to the regions dominated by its chief promoters, namely, the Vṛṣṇis, the Sātvatas, and the Yādavas-Ābhīras, had not till then spread far towards Eastern India which was the provenance of Buddhism. As against this, it was well known to Pāṇini of Takṣaśilā as also to Megasthenes, and it attracted, in course of time, even

[[P237]] foreigners like Heliodoros. Further, as mentioned above, one of the Niddesa texts (1st century B. C.), which, though of the nature of commentaries, share the authority of the Pali canon, alludes to various religious sects among which are included the worshippers of Vāsudeva and Baladeva. So far as Jainism is concerned, it seems to have been more positively responsive to Vāsudevism. The Vāsudeva-legends have markedly influenced the Jaina hagiology, particularly its concept of sixty-three śalākā-puruṣas who comprise, among others, nine Vāsudevas, nine Baladevas, and nine Prativāsudevas.

Another striking point about the Vāsudeva-religion is that the word vāsudevaka, in the sense of a Bhakta of Vāsudeva, is not attested in any available literary work.14 On the other hand, since a descriptive grammarian like Pāṇini has devoted a separate Sūtra to the morphology of that word, it must have been fairly common in his time. We are, therefore, constrained to assume that considerable literature of non-Vedic character15 existed in Pāṇini’s time but that it is now lost to us.16

Arjuna-cult

RGB has not discussed the implication of the latter member of the compound vāsudevārjunābhyām in P. IV. 3. 98. It shows that, besides the Vāsudeva religion, there also existed, in Pāṇini’s time, a religious sect centering round Arjuna. But, unlike the Vāsudeva-religion, the religious history of India knows hardly anything of the Arjuna-religion. It is suggested that P. IV. 3. 98 does not presuppose two independent religious sects—one of the Vāsudevakas and the other of the Arjunakas—but that the Sūtra refers to one single religious sect with Vāsudeva as the supreme god and Arjuna as the special devotee. Obviously, this suggestion is not supported by the wording of the Sūtra. We have to accept that, in Pāṇini’s time, there did prevail two independent religious cults, namely, the Vāsudeva-cult and the Arjuna-cult.

[[P238]] Further, from the manner in which they have been referred to by Pāṇini, it may be assumed that these two cults were closely allied to each other – the Vāsudeva-cult having been regarded, even at that time, as being superior to the Arjuna-cult. In the Ṛgvedic mythology, Viṣṇu is closely associated with Indra.17 According to Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa II. 1. 2. 11 and V. 4. 3. 7, Arjuna is a secret or mystical (guhya) name of Indra, while, in later Vaiṣṇavism, the personality and character of Vedic Viṣṇu have been merged into those of Vāsudeva.18 Therefore, the alliance between the Vāsudeva-sect, with which Pāṇini seems to have been familiar, may be said to correspond, in a way, with the alliance between Viṣṇu and Indra.19 Another prototype of such an alliance is to be seen in the religious ideology pertaining to Nara and Nārāyaṇa,20 wherein Nara may be regarded as the alter ego of Indra (of the Ṛg-veda) and Arjuna (of P. IV. 3. 98) and Nārāyaṇa as that of Viṣṇu (of the Ṛg-veda) and Vāsudeva (of P. IV. 3. 98). It would seem that, even in the initial stages of their history, the Vāsudeva-sect subdued and subsumed within itself the Arjuna-sect.21 That would account for the fact that no traces of this latter sect have been preserved either in history or literature.

Saṃkarṣaṇa-cult and Vyūhas

RGB has not said much about the Saṃkarṣaṇa-religion, though he has suggested that Saṃkarṣaṇa might have originally been a god outside the Vaiṣṇava pantheon.22 Like the Arjuna-religion, the Saṃkarṣaṇa-religion also seems to have arisen as an independent religion; but, unlike the former, its historical [[P239]] development can be reconstructed from literary and archaeological sources. In the Mahā-bhārata, Saṃkarṣaṇa or Balarāma is represented as a Vṛṣṇi hero, the elder son of Vasudeva and Rohiṇī; but he does not play any significant role in the great epic.23 The earliest literary reference to the Saṃkarṣaṇa-cult is to be found in Kauṭilīya-Artha-śāstra (4th century B. C.) XIII. 3. 67, which speaks of the spies disguised as the ascetic worshippers of Saṃkarṣaṇa. Indulgence in intoxicating drinks seems to have been a special feature of that cult. Megasthenes says that Dionysus (= Bacchus, God of wine) is represented in India by three gods, and from the further description it becomes clear that, according to the Greek writer, one of them is Saṃkarṣaṇa. In the initial stages of its evolution, the Saṃkarṣaṇa-cult was closely related to the Nāga-cult – a fact which later led to the belief that Saṃkarṣaṇa was an Avatāra of Śeṣa or Ananta-Nāga. Saṃkarṣaṇa’s connections with inebriety and the snake-cult further led to his being often identified with Rudra-Śiva in the Pāñcarātra-Saṃhitās.24 A further piece of evidence in support of the assumption that the Saṃkarṣaṇa-religion must have originated independently of the Vāsudeva-religion is the Mathurā sculpture of the second century B. C., depicting Saṃkarṣaṇa by himself.25 It is, however, not correct to say that Saṃkarṣaṇa was originally a non-Brahmanical agricultural deity which later came to be identified with Balarāma,26 for, Saṃkarṣaṇa and Balarāma nowhere appear as two separate individuals.

The worship of Saṃkarṣaṇa constituting an independent religious sect may be thus presumed to have arisen more or less contemporaneously with Vāsudevism; but its sphere of influence seems to have been rather restricted. Soon, the two independent religions, namely, the Saṃkarṣaṇa-religion and the [[P240]] Vāsudeva-religion, came to be closely allied to each other. It is this stage in the religious history which is reflected in the Mahā-bhāṣya of Patañjali, the Niddesa text, and the Ghosundi and Nanaghat inscriptions. Attention has recently been drawn also to a joint representation of Vāsudeva and Saṃkarṣaṇa belonging to the second century B. C.27 Two points need to be noted in this connection. The alliance between the Saṃkarṣaṇa-cult and the Vāsudeva-cult was not the result, as suggested by some scholars,28 of a deliberate attempt to assimilate the Nāga-cult into the Vāsudeva-religion; rather it seems to have been prompted by the historical necessity of preserving and consolidating the religious cohesion of the Vṛṣṇis – Sātvatas which would have been adversely affected if these two religious sects, which had arisen within the tribe, had been allowed to develop independently of each other. Secondly, the mention in the inscriptions of Saṃkarṣaṇa before Vāsudeva does not imply the precedence of the Saṃkarṣaṇa-religion over the Vāsudeva-religion.29 Such a mention is only formal and is presumably occasioned by the historical fact that Saṃkarṣaṇa was the elder of the two Vṛṣṇi brothers who had been deified. It may be noted that the Ghosundi inscription characterises both the gods equally as anihata, bhagavat, and sarveśvara.

Doctrine of Vyūhas

The other development in the religious history of that same period – a development which must have been independent of the first, namely, the alliance on equal terms between the Saṃkarṣaṇa-religion and the Vāsudeva-religion – was in the direction of the evolution of the doctrine of the Vyūhas. Though the doctrine of the Vyūhas, in its developed form, first occurs in the Nārāyaṇīya section (early centuries of the Christian era),30 it is adumbrated [[P241]] even in the Mahā-bhāṣya. In his commentary on Vārttika 2 of P. VI. 3. 5, Patañjali quotes the verse-quarter in the Upendra-vajrā metre, janārdanas tv ātma-caturtha eva, which can be taken to allude to the four Vyūhas. It is not clear why RGB expresses some doubt about this.31 After Saṃkarṣaṇa had been included among the Vyūhas, the Saṃkarṣaṇa-cult must have ceased to exist as an independent religious cult.

Vāsudeva and Yādava Kṛṣṇa

RGB says that Vāsudeva was also called Kṛṣṇa because his gotra was Kārṣṇāyana.32 In this connection he emphasizes the Vedic affiliations of Kṛṣṇa by pointing out that one Kṛṣṇa Āṅgirasa is the Ṛṣi of RV VIII. 74, that the same Kṛṣṇa Āṅgirasa is mentioned in KB 30. 9, and that Kṛṣṇa occurs in the Chāndogya-Upaniṣad as the pupil of Ghora Āṅgirasa. The Vedic seer Kṛṣṇa could thus have been a founder of a gotra. RGB further adds that, though Vāsudeva belonged to the Kṣatriya family of the Vṛṣṇis, he must have adopted the gotra of his Brāhmaṇa priest for sacrificial purpose.33 In support of his assumption, RGB refers to the commentary of the Ghaṭa-Jātaka which seems to suggest that Vāsudeva belonged to the Kāṇhāyana gotra. All this argument, however, is rather farfetched. Even assuming that Vāsudeva did adopt the Kārṣṇāyana gotra, one would not be able to explain why Vāsudeva was called Kṛṣṇa. He might have called himself Kārṣṇāyana, but not Kṛṣṇa. One hardly finds a person adopting the name of the founder of his gotra as his additional personal name. The commentary of the Ghaṭa-Jātaka, which too is a little confused about the gotra-name and the personal name, obviously represents a later tradition. Incidentally, Kṛṣṇa of the Chāndogya-Upaniṣad need not have been a Brāhmaṇa seer.

Another assumption, which is often made in this context, is that, from the very beginning, Kṛṣṇa was an additional personal name of Vāsudeva of the Vṛṣṇis.34 It is, however, rather unusual [[P242]] that an individual should have been originally given two equally important personal names. Pāṇini, who knows Vāsudeva as a god, nowhere alludes to Kṛṣṇa being the other name of that god. Similarly, in connection with the Vyūha-doctrine, which is associated in a distinctive manner with the Vṛṣṇi heroes, the name Kṛṣṇa is hardly ever mentioned. It is also difficult to accept the suggestion that Vṛṣṇi Vāsudeva was called Kṛṣṇa on account of his dark complexion35 and that, in course of time, the epithet became a personal name. As regards the names Vāsudeva and Kṛṣṇa, the view is sometimes put forth that Kṛṣṇa was the original name of the Vṛṣṇi hero and that Vāsudeva was just his patronym derived from his father’s name Vasudeva. This view cannot be accepted for various reasons. Firstly, Patañjali’s commentary on Vārttika 7 of P. IV. 1. 11436 indicates that Vāsudeva (and not Vasudeva) is the basic form of the name of the Vṛṣṇi hero and that the termination aṇ affixed to this original name Vāsudeva also gives the form Vāsudeva. Secondly, if Vāsudeva was really a patronym, Balarāma or Saṃkarṣaṇa, who is represented as the elder brother of Kṛṣṇa, should have been more aptly called Vāsudeva. But that is not the case. On the other hand, we find Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma being specifically referred to by their matronyms, namely, Devakīputra and Rauhiṇeya respectively. The rivalry among some Kṣatriya princes for the real divine “Vāsudevahood” also clearly goes against Vāsudeva being regarded as a patronym.37 Again the etymologies of the word vāsudeva given by the Mahā-bhārata38 and Ujjvaladatta’s gloss of Uṇādi-sūtra I. 139 would leave no doubt that Vāsudeva was regarded as the original name and not a patronym.40 Jacobi mentions the fact,41 which is particularly [[P243]] significant in the present context, namely, that the name Vāsubhadra sometimes occurs as an auxiliary of Vāsudeva.42 We have, therefore, to conclude that, just as Kṛṣṇa was not an additional personal name of Vāsudeva, Vāsudeva was not the patronym of Kṛṣṇa.

Merger of two personalities

The truth of this whole matter seems to be that Vāsudeva and Kṛṣṇa were originally two distinct personalities. As pointed out earlier, Vāsudevism must have originated at a fairly early date43 so that it could become thoroughly well established in Pāṇini’s time. Perhaps a little later than the Vāsudeva-religion, there grew another religious sect round the figure of Kṛṣṇa who had originally been the tribal hero and religious leader of the Yādavas44 but who was subsequently made into a god. This Yādava Kṛṣṇa45 may as well have been the same as Devakīputra Kṛṣṇa who is represented in Chāndogya-Upaniṣad III. 17. 1 as a pupil of Ghora Āṅgirasa and who is said to have learnt from his teacher the doctrine that man’s life is a kind of sacrifice.46 Chronology does not go against such an assumption; indeed, it supports it. Kṛṣṇa must be said to have developed his own [[P244]] philosophy (later embodied in the Bhagavad-gītā), the main tenets of which were loka-saṃgraha and renunciation in action rather than of action. When, in course of time, the Vṛṣṇis and the Yādavas, who were already related to each other, came closer together presumably for political reasons, the divine personalities of Vṛṣṇi Vāsudeva and Yādava Kṛṣṇa were merged together so as to give rise to the new supreme god, Bhagavān Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa.47 This must have happened not long after Pāṇini’s time. Kautilīya-Artha-śāstra XIV. 3 associates Kṛṣṇa with Kaṃsa, who is usually associated with Vāsudeva, thereby suggesting the identity between Kṛṣṇa and Vāsudeva. Megasthenes also seems to confirm this identity. He speaks of the “city of Kṛṣṇa” (Kleisobora = Kṛṣṇapura) in the country of Śūrasena where the worship of Vāsudeva was prevalent. In the time of the Bhagavad-gītā, and naturally also of the Mahā-bhāṣya of Patañjali,48 the names Vāsudeva and Kṛṣṇa had come to be used indiscriminately to denote the same divine personality. In the new legends which grew round this composite supreme god Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa, the name Vāsudeva came to be interpreted as the patronym of Kṛṣṇa. Some Vasudeva was imagined to have been Kṛṣṇa’s father,49 and Vṛṣṇi Saṃkarṣaṇa came to be regarded as his elder brother.50 Another hypothesis may also be hazarded in this connection. Since Vṛṣṇi Vāsudeva and Yādava Kṛṣṇa had originally been two distinct [[P245]] personalities, Vasudeva could as well have been the name of Yādava Kṛṣṇa’s father.51 Kṛṣṇa would then be Yādava (tribal name) as well as Vāsudeva (patronym). The merger of Vṛṣṇi Vāsudeva and Yādava Vāsudeva Kṛṣṇa might have been facilitated by the common element Vāsudeva.

Gopāla Kṛṣṇa and Ābhīras

Though RGB does not consider Yādava Kṛṣṇa to have been distinct from Vṛṣṇi Vāsudeva, he does believe that the Gopāla Kṛṣṇa element was introduced into Vāsudevism at a fairly late stage in the development of the latter.52 He points out that no reference to Gopāla Kṛṣṇa occurs in the epigraphical records like the Ghosundi and the Nanaghat inscriptions; nor does it occur in the Mahā-bhāṣya of Patañjali or even in the Nārāyaṇīya section of the Mahā-bhārata. No doubt, in the Sabhā-parvan, Śiśupāla is represented as referring tauntingly to Kṛṣṇa’s ’exploits’ in the Gokula, like the killing of Pūtanā,53 but RGB regards that passage as an interpolation. Similarly, he accepts the interpretation of Kṛṣṇa’s name Govinda, given in the Ādi-parvan and the Śānti-parvan, as “finder of the earth”, thereby rejecting any suggestion regarding Kṛṣṇa having been connected with the cows. However, he hastens to add that, more probably, the name means “finder of the cows.” Finally he concludes that the story of Kṛṣṇa’s boyhood in the Gokula was unknown till about the beginning of the Christian era, that it occurs in its full-fledged form only in the Hari-vaṃśa, the Vāyu-Purāṇa, and the Bhāgavata-Purāṇa, that some of the elements in the Gopāla Kṛṣṇa mythology, which clearly betray Christian influence, such as, the worship of the boy-god and the story of his humble birth, his reputed father’s knowledge that he was not his son, and the massacre of the innocents, were brought into India by the Ābhīras when they migrated into this country in the first century A. D., and that the Ābhīras possibly brought with them also the name Christ, which probably led to the identification of the boy-god with Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa.

[[P246]] There can be little doubt that the religion centering round the cowherd child-god Kṛṣṇa originated among the nomadic cowherd community of the Ābhīras.54 In the Padma-Purāṇa, the supreme god is represented as announcing that he would be born among the Ābhīras in his eighth incarnation.55 Similarly, in the Hari-vaṃśa56 and the Bāla-carita57 of Bhāsa, Kṛṣṇa is said to have been brought up in a ghoṣa; and, according to the Amara-kośa,58 ābhīra-palli is a synonym of ghoṣa. The religious cult of Gopāla Kṛṣṇa abjured the Indra-dominated Vedic religion (as is evidenced by the Govardhana-episode) and promoted religious sublimation of sensuous love (cf. the relationship between Kṛṣṇa and the Gopīs).

Critique of Christian influence

We need not here go into the question of the origin of the Ābhīras. Whether they were a foreign tribe59 or an aboriginal one living in Panjab,60 there is sufficient evidence to show that they were in India already in the third century B.C.61 So RGB’s suggestion regarding the Christian influences brought in by the Ābhīras cannot be sustained.62 Again, the so-called similarities between the legends pertaining to Gopāla Kṛṣṇa and Christ, to which RGB refers, are patently superficial. Attention may also be drawn to a Mathurā sculpture, depicting Vasudeva as crossing the Yamunā with the baby Kṛṣṇa in a basket, which has been assigned to the Kuṣāṇa period.63 This may be taken to prove [[P247]] that the legend regarding Kṛṣṇa’s transference to Gokula (which, in its turn, is the result of the merger of the Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa-religion and the Gopāla-Kṛṣṇa-religion) had already been in common vogue even in the first century A.D. Similarly a figure of Govardhanadhara Kṛṣṇa in red sandstone, from Mathurā, is assigned to the same period.64 Bhāsa, who has to be assigned to a still earlier period, portrays, in his Bāla-carita, various episodes in Kṛṣṇa’s life in the cow-settlement. As for RGB’s contention that the passage in the Sabhā-parvan, mentioned above, may be an interpolation, the evidence of the Critical Edition of the Mahā-bhārata goes counter to it. Indeed, there are a few other passages in the Mahā-bhārata which betray knowledge of Kṛṣṇa’s life in the Gokula.65 All the same, one does not fail to get the impression that the redactors of the Mahā-bhārata must have made deliberate attempts to suppress many of the episodes of Gopāla Kṛṣṇa’s boyhood in the Gokula, particularly his amours with the Gopīs, presumably because those redactors wanted specially to highlight Kṛṣṇa’s character as a divine teacher of new ethics.66

Non-Vedic character

Just as the religion of Gopāla Kṛṣṇa, in its original form, does not show any traces of Christian influence (as suggested by RGB), so too it cannot be shown to have originated from Vedic sources.67 The word gopā is hardly ever used in the ṚV in the specific sense of ‘cowherd’;68 and the word gāvaḥ, when associated with Viṣṇu, does not necessarily mean ‘cows’.69 Reference to Gobala Vārṣṇya in the Taittirīya-Saṃhitā and the Jaiminīya-Upaniṣad-Brāhmaṇa70 also does not indicate the Vedic [[P248]] affiliations of Ābhīra Gopāla Kṛṣṇa. As against this, Indra is often described in the ṚV as govid (= finder of cows), but Gopāla Kṛṣṇa, as we have seen, asks his followers to desist from Indra-worship and to regard cows as their deities. The original non-Vedic character of the Gopāla-Kṛṣṇa-cult is thus beyond doubt.

Synthesis into new Kṛṣṇaism

It would seem that the Gopāla-Kṛṣṇa-religion of the Ābhīras was, in course of time, engrafted on the organically united Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa-religion of the Vṛṣṇis-Yādavas.71 This must have happened some time before the third century B.C.; for, the Bhagavad-gītā contains the words govinda72 and keśi-niṣūdana73 which are reminiscent of Kṛṣṇa’s boyhood days in the Gokula. Similarly, Kātyāyana (3rd century B.C.) found it necessary to add a Vārttika to P. III. 1. 138 to explain the morphology of the name Govinda74 which must have become fairly common in his days. The words dāmodara and govinda, which definitely belong to the Gopāla-Kṛṣṇa religious complex, also occur in the Baudhāyana-Dharma-sūtra (II. 5. 24) of about the same period.

The amalgamation of the Vāsudeva-religion of the Vṛṣṇis, the Kṛṣṇa-religion of the Yādavas, and the Gopāla-religion of the Ābhīras gave rise to what may be called a new Kṛṣṇaism. If, as pointed out earlier, Vāsudevism represented the first phase of classical Vaiṣṇavism, this new Kṛṣṇaism represented its second (and perhaps most outstanding) phase. New legendary links were forged whereby Vāsudeva, Kṛṣṇa, and Gopāla were integrated into a single homogeneous mythological pattern. Vasudeva, who, as the result of the merger of the Vāsudeva-religion and the Kṛṣṇa-religion, had already come to be regarded as Kṛṣṇa’s [[P249]] father, was now represented as having transferred his infant son Kṛṣṇa to the care of his friend Nanda, the Ābhīra leader of a ghoṣa, with a view to guarding him against the misfortune of merciless slaughter which his earlier children had suffered at the hands of his brother-in-law Kaṃsa. There, in the Gokula, the boy Kṛṣṇa grew in the company of Gopas and Gopīs as Gopāla Kṛṣṇa (Ābhīra element). From there, he (with his elder brother Saṃkarṣaṇa-Balarāma) went over to Mathurā, killed Kaṃsa, and thereby put an end to the latter’s despotic rule (Vṛṣṇi element). Thereafter, Kṛṣṇa migrated to Dvārakā to found and foster the kingdom of the Yādavas (Yādava element). He also played the role of the friend and mentor of the Pāṇḍavas. Such was the composite mythology of Kṛṣṇaism. The religio-philosophico-ethical doctrine of this Kṛṣṇaism is embodied in the Bhagavad-gītā.

Assimilation of Vedic Viṣṇu

The third century B. C. was the most eventful period in the history of Vaiṣṇavism. As we have seen, Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism originated and developed as an essentially non-Vedic religious sect. Other heterodox religions, particularly Buddhism, had also been flourishing, so much so that, in the third century B. C., Buddhism reached almost the peak of its power. It must have, indeed, inspired the fear that it would soon overwhelm not only the declining Vedic religion but also other non-Vedic religious sects. Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism, therefore, prepared itself to meet this serious challenge. It did so in two ways. Firstly it rallied together all the religious forces akin to it. The amalgamation of the Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa-religion and the Gopāla-Kṛṣṇa-religion and the consequent bringing into being of the new Kṛṣṇaism, to which a reference has been made above, was an important step in that direction. The other line of action, which it followed, may be said to have been rather unexpected. Non-Vedic in origin and development, Kṛṣṇaism now sought affiliation with Vedism—howsoever tenuous and formal that affiliation might be—so that it could thereby make itself acceptable to the still not inconsiderable orthodox elements among the people. The rear-guards of the languishing Vedism, on their part, had also been wanting [[P250]] to reclaim its former position. For one thing, they tried to revive, reorganize, and revitalise the traditional Vedic way of life and thought; the literary monuments of this movement were the Śrauta-, the Dharma-, and the Gṛhya-Sūtras. As an additional measure, they must have welcomed the prospect of at least some Vedic elements being preserved through Kṛṣṇaism. The rapport which Kṛṣṇaism was striving to establish with Vedism was thus expected to prove beneficial both to Vedism and Kṛṣṇaism. This is the background on which the Viṣṇu-element and the Nārāyaṇa-element came to be merged into Kṛṣṇaism. RGB has dealt with this merger, but he does not give its historical rationale.

We need not here analyse in detail the Viṣṇu-mythology in the Ṛg-veda.75 Suffice it to say that it symbolises an important motif in the evolution of Vedic mythology, namely, hierarchisation of popular religion. Viṣṇu was originally a popular divinity of fertility and growth, conceived (as in several primitive mythologies) in the form of bird.76 However, popular pressure seems to have rendered it necessary to admit Viṣṇu in the hieratic Vedic pantheon. The artificiality of this mythological elevation of Viṣṇu is indicated by the forced manner in which he has been brought into association with Indra, who was, as it were, the philosopher’s stone in the matter of hierarchisation. And, when Viṣṇu was thus being related to Indra, the Vedic poet-priest took care to suppress the original fertility traits in Viṣṇu’s character and to superimpose on him the conventional solar character. RGB is, indeed, right in not stressing the Ṛgvedic Viṣṇu’s identity with the sun. The superimposition of solar character on Viṣṇu was presumably facilitated by the common symbol of ‘bird’ which represented both the fertility-divinity and the sun. In the course of the further evolution of the Viṣṇu-mythology, Viṣṇu came to be regarded as the supreme (parama) god from the ritualistic point of view.77 It was, however, his character as a [[P251]] god of fertility and growth which appealed most to the popular imagination and which consequently served as the most convenient point of contact between Kṛṣṇaism (particularly its Gopāla-Kṛṣṇa aspect) and Vedism. It is also not unlikely that the popular understanding – or rather misunderstanding – of the Ṛgvedic Viṣṇu’s epithet gopā and of the gāvaḥ in his highest abode78 also helped the identification of the popular god Kṛṣṇa with the Vedic Viṣṇu.

Kṛṣṇaisation of Viṣṇu

The assimilation of the Viṣṇu-element79 by Kṛṣṇaism, resulting in Kṛṣṇa being identified with Viṣṇu, must have occurred before the final redaction of the Bhagavad-gītā. In the Bhagavad-gītā, Lord Kṛṣṇa is addressed as Viṣṇu.80 Attention may also be drawn to Arjuna’s prayer to Kṛṣṇa that he should again appear before him in ’that same four-armed form’.81 Further, as RGB has pointed out,82 the viśva-rūpa manifested to Arjuna by Lord Kṛṣṇa83 is almost similar to the vaiṣṇava-rūpa mentioned in the Anugītā.84 This fact would confirm Kṛṣṇa’s identification with Viṣṇu. Again, [[P252]] in the Baudhāyana-Dharma-sūtra,85 which belongs to about the same period as the Bhagavad-gītā, Viṣṇu is called Govinda and Dāmodara,86 and in the Taittirīya-Āraṇyaka,87 which is a little posterior to the Bhagavad-gītā, he is identified with Vāsudeva and Nārāyaṇa. The identity of Vāsudeva with Viṣṇu is also suggested by the fact that, in the Besnagar inscription,88 Vāsudeva’s column is called Garuḍa-column. Further, according to WEBER,89 the fact that, in Patañjali’s commentary on Vārttika 6 of P. III. 1. 26, the episodes of Kaṃsa-vadha (of which Kṛṣṇa is the hero) and Balibandha (of which Viṣṇu is the hero) have been mentioned together, shows that Kṛṣṇa and Viṣṇu stood in close relationship with each other.90 Similarly, it is significant that a coin of the Vṛṣṇi-rājanya-gaṇa of the first century B. C. bears the two emblems of Viṣṇu—cakra on the reverse and Garuḍa on the [[observe|obverse]].91 Incidentally it may be mentioned that, in the Mahā-bhārata, the name Viṣṇu occurs less frequently than the names, Vāsudeva, Kṛṣṇa, and Nārāyaṇa.

Two points need to be specially noted in connection with the assimilation of the Viṣṇu element by Kṛṣṇaism. Firstly, when Viṣṇu came to be assimilated into Kṛṣṇaism, there occurred a vital change in his character. In the Ṛgvedic mythology, Viṣṇu is represented as a subordinate ally of Indra. In Kṛṣṇaism, Viṣṇu not only supersedes Indra but, in his role as Kṛṣṇa, he actively antagonises him. The legends concerning the Govardhana mountain and the Pārijāta tree would confirm this.92 Secondly, it is not quite correct to characterise the assimilation of the Viṣṇu- [[P253]] element into Kṛṣṇaism as the ‘Brahmanisation’ of Kṛṣṇaism,93 for, it actually resulted in the Kṛṣṇaisation of Viṣṇu rather than in the Viṣṇuisation of Kṛṣṇa. No significant Brahmanic traits are seen to have been introduced into Kṛṣṇaism together with the Viṣṇu-element. Indeed, the position of Viṣṇu in this entire newly evolved religious complex is such as would hardly justify that religious complex being designated as Vaiṣṇavism. The only indication of Viṣṇu’s predominance is to be found in the newly propagated belief that Kṛṣṇa was an avatāra or incarnation of Viṣṇu.

The Avatāra doctrine

The avatāras constitute a special characteristic of Vaiṣṇavism. RGB has noted the various references to avatāras,94 but he has not discussed the avatāra-theory as such. The avatāra-theory is essentially purpose-oriented. In the Bhagavad-gītā, the Lord declares that, whenever Dharma (righteousness) languishes and Adharma (unrighteousness) thrives, he incarnates himself. The moral aspect of the avatāra-theory is emphasized by suggesting that god comes down to man in order that man may strive to become god by following his example.95 The avatāra-theory also facilitated the transcendent being brought into direct relation with the phenomenal world. It promoted the doctrines of Bhakti and grace by providing their very sine [[que|qua]] non, namely, a personal god. Further, the avatāra-theory gave the psychologically much needed assurance that god was not merely an indifferent and uninterested spectator of the world-process which he had initiated but that he was responsibly involved in it. From the point of view of the history of Hinduism, the avatāra-theory may be said to have helped the synthesis of various religious sects which that religion had sought to accomplish. It integrated into a single scheme the presiding divinities of those sects by regarding them [[P254]] as the incarnations of the one supreme god.96 Incidentally, it may be pointed out that, among all the avatāras, Kṛṣṇa is glorified as the pūrṇāvatāra.

The Bhagavad-gītā

The Bhagavad-gītā reflects the stage of Kṛṣṇaism after it had assimilated the Viṣṇu-element. RGB gives a detailed chapterwise summary of the BG. He also discusses at some length the sources of the religion of the BG.97 RGB is too prone to trace the origin of most of the teachings of the BG to the Upaniṣads. Thus, for instance, he connects (not quite convincingly!) the BG doctrine of Karmayoga with the insistence of the Īśa-Upaniṣad (st. 2) on man’s doing actions resolutely for a hundred years coupled with the idea of non-contamination resulting from an elevated state of mind as spoken of in Chāndogya-Upaniṣad IV. 14. 3, Bṛhad-āraṇyaka-Upaniṣad IV. 23, and Maitrī-Upaniṣad VI. 20. Further, according to RGB, the BG derives the attributes of the Supreme, the concept of brahma-nirvāṇa, and even the teaching of Bhakti from the Upaniṣadic sources. It would, however, seem that, in the matter of essential attitude and doctrine, the BG represents a kind of reaction against the prevailing Vedic ideology. It must be said to have adequately responded to the needs of the hour. Broadly speaking, the major Upaniṣads, which mark the acme of the religio-philosophical culture of the Vedas, teach that the ultimate goal of human life is the realisation of the essential identity of the individual soul and the universal soul which results from the complete withdrawal from the fundamentally unreal phenomenal world. The Upaniṣadic attitude is thus individualistic and, to a large extent, negative. As against this, the BG emphasizes the ideal of loka-saṃgraha – of the stability, solidarity, and progress of the society. The society can function properly only on the principle of the ethical interdependence of its various constituents. Man must, therefore, see to it that, as an essential constituent of society, he furthers this process of loka-saṃgraha through an active awareness of his social obligations [[P255]] (svadharma). The BG countenances neither the complete abnegation of action (saṃnyāsa) as implied in the Upaniṣads nor the mechanical observance of the ritual practices (karma-kāṇḍa) enjoined by the Brāhmaṇas. The Karmayoga, which is verily the central theme of the BG, tries to combine the ideals of loka-saṃgraha and mokṣa by stressing the anāsakti-yoga, that is, renunciation in action and not of action. The elaborate ritualism of the Brāhmaṇas and the contemplative spiritualism of the Upaniṣads were both beyond the common man. The BG, therefore, emphasizes the way of Bhakti for a personal god, which, as we have seen, was the very starting point of Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism. But it seeks to harmonize Bhakti with Karmayoga. The true Karmayogin is also a true Bhakta, for, by following his svadharma, the former devotedly participates in the divine project. The BG cannot boast of any distinct philosophical system of its own; it has eschewed the metaphysical dogmatism of the prevailing schools of philosophy and has sought to evolve a loose philosophical synthesis.98 Synthesis in all fields was, verily, the watchword of the new Kṛṣṇaism. Another significant trait of Kṛṣṇaism, which is not reflected in the BG but which is clearly brought out by Kṛṣṇa’s character as represented in the Mahā-bhārata, is its new political ethics which insists on the urgency of attaining the end, which one considers to be right, by any means even if they sometimes do not appear to be so right.99

Presumably, the religio-philosophical teachings of Kṛṣṇaism were originally embodied in an independent popular text. This text was later interpolated, obviously after suitable modifications, into the bardic-historical poem Jaya, in the form of the Bhagavad-gītā, whereby it served as the cornerstone of the Kṛṣṇaite superstructure which transformed the Jaya into the Bhārata.100

Nārāyaṇa

The assimilation of the Nārāyaṇa-element into Kṛṣṇaism,101 which is generally regarded as the second major factor in the process of the so-called brahmanization of Kṛṣṇaism, does not seem to have been known to the Bhagavad-gītā. The name Nārāyaṇa does not occur in the Bhagavad-gītā, nor does the doctrine of the Vyūhas which is believed to have been taught to Nārada by the great Being in the Śveta-dvīpa (that is, Nārāyaṇa).102 RGB’s suggestion that the doctrine of the Vyūhas may have been implied in the Bhagavad-gītā VII. 4–5, wherein a reference is made to the eightfold prakṛti of the Lord,103 is not at all convincing. It would seem that, very soon after the final redaction of the Bhagavad-gītā, Nārāyaṇa became an essential constituent of Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism. The Taittirīya-Āraṇyaka X. 11 clearly identifies Nārāyaṇa, Vāsudeva, and Viṣṇu.104 The association of Nārāyaṇa with Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism must have become well established since then. The Ghosundi inscription of Sarvatāta speaks of the nārāyaṇa-vāṭaka105 for the place of worship of Saṃkarṣaṇa and Vāsudeva. In the Mahā-bhārata XII. 321. 16–17, the supreme god is said to have manifested himself in the four forms—Nara, Nārāyaṇa, Hari, and Kṛṣṇa. Nārāyaṇa is also glorified there as sātvata-dharma-goptā (XII. 322. 5). He declares that he can be seen only by those who devoutly follow the ekāntika-dharma (XII. 326. 12). In the Deva-bodhi-sattva-śāstra, Āryadeva (second century A. D.) quotes certain sayings of [[P257]] Nārāyaṇa which are quite similar to what Lord Kṛṣṇa has said in the Bhagavad-gītā.106 Similarly, the episode described in the Mahā-bhārata III. 186. 39 ff. of Mārkaṇḍeya’s having entered into the mouth of Nārāyaṇa and having seen the entire universe existing within that god’s body would remind one of Arjuna’s viśva-rūpa-darśana as described in the Bhagavad-gītā XI. It may also be pointed out that the Ājīvikas, who flourished in the sixth-fifth centuries B. C. as a non-Vedic religious sect but who came to be later closely associated with the Vāsudevakas, were characterised as nārāyaṇāśrita.107

Solar adoration?

The name Nārāyaṇa is variously explained. On the face of it, it looks like a gotra-name (like Kātyāyana or Vātsyāyana) derived from the name Nara. Nārāyaṇa was accordingly regarded as a sage born in the family of another sage called Nara. It was further suggested that both these sages, who were later deified, were originally great sun-worshippers, which fact facilitated the later identification of Nārāyaṇa with Viṣṇu. However, in the Mahā-bhārata, it is Nara who is represented as having been born as the result of the austerities of Nārāyaṇa. There is also no evidence to suggest their connection with sun-worship. Further, doubt is expressed as to the propriety of the highest god being the descendant of any one else.108 According to Manu-smṛti I. 10, Nārāyaṇa was so called because his primary abode (pūrva ayana) was waters (nārāḥ).109 But there Nārāyaṇa is obviously represented as being identical with Brahmā-Prajā-pati rather than with Viṣṇu. BHANDARKAR accepts the formation of the word nārāyaṇa according to P. IV. 1. 99, but explains its meaning in quite an [[P258]] ingenious way. He understands nāra in the sense of a collection of naras or men, and ayana in the sense of a resting place or goal. The god Nārāyaṇa is thus the resting place or goal of men.110 BHANDARKAR refers, in this context, to the statement made by Keśava or Hari to Arjuna that he is known as the resting place or goal of men (narāṇām).111 The form nāra is no doubt possible, but it is by no means common. BHANDARKAR’s explanation is, accordingly, farfetched and, therefore, not very convincing.112 It would rather seem that Nara and Nārāyaṇa originally constituted one single personality conceived as having been made up of two closely related aspects and that the essential idea underlying it was that of the “cosmic male” or Puruṣa.113

Puruṣa-Nārāyaṇa

RGB’s analysis of the Nārāyaṇīya114 is excellent; but it does not throw much light on how and why the Nārāyaṇa-element might have been merged into Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism. The several references to the ascetics-yogins associated with Nārāyaṇa in Badari or Śveta-dvīpa115 would indicate that the Nara-Nārāyaṇa cult had originated independently of the Veda; but the connection of Nārāyaṇa with the Indus valley civilization cannot be validly [[P259]] established.116 Tradition invests the Nārāyaṇa-cult with great antiquity, for, Nārāyaṇa is described as yo ‘sau nārāyaṇo nāma pūrveṣām api pūrva-jaḥ.117 It is not unlikely that this very same Nārāyaṇa was adapted into the Vedic ideology in the form of Puruṣa Nārāyaṇa of the Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa.118 According to the Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa XII. 3. 4, Prajā-pati urged Puruṣa Nārāyaṇa to perform a sacrifice. He did so and, as the result of it, he “placed himself in all the worlds, in all the gods, in all the Vedas, and in all the vital airs, and they were placed in him.”119 In the Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa XIII. 6. 1, Puruṣa Nārāyaṇa is said to have performed a Puruṣa-medha extending over five nights whereby he superseded all beings and himself became identical with the All. Presumably, it was this latter reference which gave rise to the tradition of Nārāyaṇa being associated with the famous cosmological hymn, the Puruṣa-sūkta (ṚV X. 90),120 as its Ṛṣi. But what seems to have facilitated the merger of Nārāyaṇa into Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism (which was intended to serve the same purpose as the merger of Viṣṇu) was not so much his cosmic character and function as his dual personality as Puruṣa Nārāyaṇa. Correspondence could be easily established among the four pairs, namely, Nara-Nārāyaṇa of Badarikāśrama, Puruṣa-Nārāyaṇa of the Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa, Indra-Viṣṇu of the Ṛgvedic mythology, and Arjuna-Vāsudeva of Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism.

Pāñcarātra and Bhāgavatas

Once Nārāyaṇa was assimilated into Vāsudevism-Kṛṣṇaism, he came to be represented as the propounder of a specific aspect [[P260]] of Vaiṣṇavism, namely, the Pāñcarātra.121 This was presumably due to the reference in the Śata-patha-Brāhmaṇa to the Pāñcarātra Sattra which Puruṣa Nārāyaṇa is said to have performed (see supra). However, neither the Nārāyaṇīya122 nor any other part of the Mahā-bhārata gives a consistent and detailed account of the Pāñcarātra doctrine. Since most of the literature on Pāñcarātra—textual as well as expository—was published after RGB’s VŚMRS,123 RGB’s treatment of that aspect of Vaiṣṇavism is understandably not very detailed. For instance, he does not discuss the various meanings of Pāñcarātra.124 Whatever might have been the original significance of that term, there should be no doubt that the doctrine was closely related to the historically authentic Vṛṣṇi heroes. An inscription from the Mora well [[P261]] (about seven miles from Mathurā), belonging to the time of the Śaka chief Sodāsa (first century A.D.) speaks of bhagavatāṃ vṛṣṇīnāṃ pañca-vīrāṇāṃ pratimāḥ installed in a stone temple by a lady named Tosā. These five vīras must be the same as the five vaṃśa-vīras of the Vṛṣṇis mentioned in the Vāyu-Purāṇa 97. 1–2, namely, Saṃkarṣaṇa, Vāsudeva, Pradyumna, Sāmba, and Aniruddha.125 It seems most likely that each of these Vṛṣṇi heroes originally had his own independent religious sect. When attempts were being made to consolidate classical Vaiṣṇavism, these various sects were presumably integrated into one single religio-philosophical pattern.126

It may be incidentally pointed out here that, though various sects of Vaiṣṇavism have been mentioned, the two most prominent are the Bhāgavatas and the Pāñcarātrins. Attempts are sometimes made to emphasize the distinction between these two sects by suggesting that the Pāñcarātrins were the worshippers of Nārāyaṇa while the Bhāgavatas were the worshippers of Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa,127 or that the Pāñcarātrins were the followers of the Tāntric Vaiṣṇavism while the Bhāgavatas were the followers of the Brahmanic Vaiṣṇavism, or that the Pañca-rātrins accepted the doctrine of Vyūhas while the Bhāgavatas accepted the doctrine of Avatāras.128

[[P262]]

Later Vaiṣṇavism and Ācāryas

There is little in the remaining portion of RGB’s treatment of Vaiṣṇavism that calls for any special comment or amplification.129 Among other things, he points out that, from about the first century B.C. up to the time when the Guptas rose to power in the first quarter of the fourth century A.D., there are no epigraphical or sculptural traces of any Brahmanic religious system, and then presents a brief chronological survey, mainly on the basis of inscriptions, of the development of Vaiṣṇavism from the fourth to the eleventh century, ‘principally in accordance with the mode professed by the Bhāgavatas’.130 He further states that, in the original Rāmāyaṇa, Rāma Dāśarathi was represented as a human hero and that it was only in the early centuries A.D. that he came to be regarded as an avatāra of Viṣṇu.131 When, however, he says that there is no evidence of the Rāma-cult as such till the eleventh century A.D.,132 one finds it difficult to agree with him, for, one is then naturally reminded of the mention of Rāmagirisvāmin in the Rithpur copperplate inscription of Prabhāvatīguptā (about 407 A.D.) and of Citrakūṭasvāmin (= Rāma) in the Gadhava Daśāvatāra temple inscription of 468 A.D., of the rules regarding the construction of the image of Rāma given in Varāhamihira’s Bṛhat-saṃhitā (c. 550 A.D.), and of the Kerala king Kulaśekhara (8th century A.D.) who was a special devotee of Rāma.

RGB speaks of the two classes of Vaiṣṇava votaries in South India—the Ālvārs and the Ācāryas (the two main tenets of these latter being Bhakti and the rejection of the doctrine of Māyā). But it is difficult to agree with him in his suggestion that some of the teachings of Rāmānuja show the influence of Christianity.133

[[P263]] His judgement that, when the female element is idolised and made the object of special worship (as in the case of Rādhā), the religious cult becomes debased and disgusting corruption ensues134 is also open to criticism.135
