HAYASĪRṢAPAÑCARĀTRA ĀDI-KĀNDA

INTRODUCTION

[[Pi]]

The Pañcarātra System

The Pañcarātra is a sect of the Vaiṣṇavas the earliest reference to which may be found in the Satapatha Brāhmaṇa (xiii. 6. 1.)1 in which the ‘puruṣa Nārāyaṇa’ is said to have conceived the idea of ‘pañcarātra satra’ or a ‘sacrificial rite to be continued for five days.’ In the ‘pañcarātra’ circle, however, the word ‘rātra’ came to be interpreted differently. The apocryphal Nāradīya takes the word ‘rātra’ to mean ‘knowledge’ and speaks of five kinds of ‘rātras’, namely, Tattva (Ontology or Cosmology), Muktiprada (Liberation), Bhaktiprada (Devotion), Yaugika (Yoga or meditation) and Vaiseṣika (the objects of senses). Our text seems to represent a different school of thought according to which the word ‘rātra’ means ’night’ which is equated with the five Mahābhūtas and which holds that the Supreme Being is above all the qualities (nirañjana), and being known causes salvation by dint of which man becomes identical with Viṣṇu himself. The text, therefore, that imparts this knowledge of the Supreme Being and helps to distinguish the ’nights’ from the Ultimate Reality is known as Pañcarātra (Hayaśīrṣa, I. 4. 1.-3). The Agnipurāṇa, a Puranic text of the Bhāgavatas of the Pañcarātra sect, however, speaks of a ‘saptarātra’ also but the term has not been explained there (Agni, 39. 1.)

In the Mahābhārata2 (XII. 338. 4.) the word ‘pāñcarātrika’ is used as an epithet of Viṣṇu and Pañcarātra also occurs in XII. 349. 1. The Harṣacarita of Bana (7th cen.) names in its Ucchvāsa VII the Pañcarātras along with the Bhagavatas. The ‘caturvyūha’3 or the quadruple emanations of Viṣṇu, namely, Kṛṣṇa, Vasudeva or Nārāyaṇa,

and the Mahābhāṣya of Patañjali (C. 150 B.C.) in the passage—“Janardanaś cātmacaturtha eva” (under, VI. 3. 6.)—has a clear reference to it. The ‘caturvyūha’ has been included in the group of ‘puruṣāvatāras’ in the Bhāgavata (I. 3.).4 Hayaśīrṣa has been referred to in the Mahābhārata (XII. 339. 60.). The Agnipurāṇa depicts Hayagrīva as the speaker in its chapter 39 and two subsequent chapters. It also praises the gift of the manuscript of Pañcarātra (79. 63.) and recommends for the worship of Hayagrīva (XLII. 24.). It is also interesting to note here that the Hayagrīva form of Viṣṇu used to be worshipped in temples and the Śilpa text of Śrīkumāra5 (16th cen.) describes for the image-makers how to make the image of that deity. Hayagrīva or Hayaśīrṣa stands as the central figure in the legends of rescue of the Vedas from the waters of the great ‘deluge’ which is described in our text (I. 1. 9.) as ’ekārṇavajala’6 and the same expression may be found in the Mahābhārata and also in the inscription of Rudradaman. In this connection our text refers to the Madhukaitabha legend which is narrated also in the Mārkaṇḍeyapurāṇa in a substantially different form. The features of the Hayagrīva image have been described in our text as having four arms wielding mace, disc, lotus, and bow, moonwhite in his complexion and his head is like that of a horse. The Śāradātilaka,7 however, describes the same deity with two arms only and Rāghavabhaṭṭa,7 its commentator, has four arms of the god wielding rosary, lotus, book and ‘varamudrā’ on the authority probably of the Śāṇkarakalpa. The Garuḍapurāṇa8 echoes our text. The Śilparatna makes him white-complexioned, seated on a throne and explaining to the disciples with ‘vyākhyānamudrā’. But the Agnipurāṇa (46. 7.)

[[Piii]] in course of describing the ‘Śālagrāmamūrtis’ has blue-coloured Hayagrīva. The Caturvargacintāmaṇi9 makes ‘Hayānana’ identical with the Sāmaveda having two arms, holding rosary and conch and blue-complexioned, and the Viṣṇudharmottara (III. 8.) also speaks of the Hayagrīva image in blue garments and placing his feet on the hands of the image of goddess Earth. All these variations clearly indicate the influence of different sectarian conceptions of the icon but the chronology of these developments is not certain.

The Ahirbudhnya-Saṁhitā holds the ‘Pañcarātra-mārga’ as superior to the ‘Veda-mārga’ and in some Pañcarātra texts undue emphasis is laid on some such rites as are manifestly non-Vedic. These possibly created a sharp reaction in the Vedic parlour and consequently we see that the Kūrmapurāṇa (I. 12. 266-68; II. 16. 15-16 and elsewhere) condemns the Pañcarātras along with the followers of the Tantras. The Brahmapurāṇa cited by Yāmunācārya in his Āgamaprāmāṇya (p. 8.) states that the ‘Sātvatas’ identified with the Vṛṣṇis were mercenary worshippers in temples and according to Yāmunācārya they were the followers of Pañcarātra and they were popularly nicknamed as ‘Devalakas’. In his Bṛhatsaṁhitā (60. 19.) Varāhamihira also refers to the Bhāgavatas as worshippers in Viṣṇu temples. Manu, Yājñavalkya and Āśvalāyana are openly antagonistic to the Pañcarātras and in Manu, X. 23. Sātvatas are described as low-caste people of hybrid origin having no sacred thread. Kumārila in his Tantravārttika and the Vedāntins (Brahmasūtra, II. 2. 43-45.) refute the Pañcarātra doctrines. The Mahābhārata (XII. 335-349.) tells us that the Citraśikhaṇḍins propounded the Pañcarātra system and made it equal to the Vedas. This has been interpreted by some as a sly hint that makes the Pañcarātra non-Vedic, if not anti-Vedic. Yāmunācārya also records an argument according to which the Pañcarātra is non-Vedic as it admits of the twofold systems, Vaidika and Tantrika.10 There are also many other reasons that made the Brahmanical tradition antagonistic to the Pañcarātras. They freely initiated the Śūdras and the women and allowed them to worship for

Pañcarātra alone, which implies that the ‘ācārya’ of the Pañcarātras may be ignorant of the Vedas. (HS., I. 4. 5-9). This clearly shows that the Vedas are not of much importance in the eyes of the followers of Pañcarātra though in the rituals they employ various Vedic mantras to grace the occasion (HS., I. 11. 28-30.). The worship of the ’linga’, deities of the ‘Śakti’ cult and the Puranic gods like ‘Skanda’ and others tend to suggest that the old ‘Bhagavata’ sect of which the Pañcarātra is an offshoot, made an attempt to integrate the Tantric and the Puranic systems also into their own tenets, of course, with a strong Viṣṇuite bias, by the passage of time.

[[Pv]]

Literature

Literature of the school: The ancient literature of the Pañcarātra school appears to be a vast one and Schrader11 gives a list of about 215 texts on the Pañcarātra. Our text (I. 2. 2-6.) gives a list of 25 authoritative texts of the Pañcarātra along with an account of some other texts that are common to all of the Bhāgavata sects (I. 2. 8-9.). Agnipurāṇa says ditto to the list and the Mantrakaumudī of the celebrated Maithila scholar Devanatha Tarkapañcānana (16th cen.) reproduces the same. The Hayaśīrṣa is held to be the ‘ādya’ (the best or the earliest) of all these texts. The original text is said to have been narrated by Brahmā to Mahāvara and the sage Mārkaṇḍeya entreated by Bhṛgu reproduces the old text to him in the form of the present text. Yāmunācārya12 informs us that, according to the Mahābhārata and the Skandapurāṇa,13 Keśava is the ‘vaktā’ or the first narrator of the Pañcarātra. Possibly this relates to the school and not the text.

Nature, contents and extent

The Hayaśīrṣa Pañcarātra is written thoroughly in the Puranic style and language and includes as such a number of un-Paninian words in the fashion of the Puranas, a list of which we propose to append at the end of the last volume. The whole work is written in the verses of ‘anuṣṭubh’ metre interspersed with some ‘mantra’ portions in prose. The diction is generally graceful and technical terms are freely used specially in connection with sculptural and architectural matters, but these terms are conventional. The text is complete in four books (Kāṇḍas) and they are: Ādi, Samkarṣaṇa, Lainga and Saura. Each of these Kandas are divided into several sections named ‘patalas’. The whole work is said to be a collection of 12,000 (‘dvādaśasāhasrika’) ‘granthas’ and possibly not of the verses as supposed by some scholars. A grantha (also called a ‘śloka’), according to the scribal tradition, is made of 32 syllables and the text available to us contains approximately 11,538 such granthas. If, however, the expression ‘dvādaśasāhasrika’ is used with reference to the verses, then we must have to presume that almost half of the text is now lost to us.

The text describes itself as a ‘pratiṣṭhātantra’ (I. 1. 28.; I. 3. 14.) and mainly deals with such topics as the details of Architecture in relation to construction and reconstruction of temples, assembly halls and such other structures, mostly in conformity with the Northern school, installation and construction of images, copying and donation of manuscripts, erection of banners, dedication of wells and tanks, salvage of wrecked ones and various other topics connected with all these things. Philosophical topics came up only incidentally and these are dealt with in a succinct manner.+++(5)+++

The text is looked upon as an authoritative work on ‘smṛti’ and the Vaisnavas of Bengal appear to have a special fascination for it. Halāyudha and Raghunandana, the great smṛti scholar of Bengal (16th cen.) in his Mathapratiṣṭhātattva14 cite our text in connection with the measurement of the flag-pole etc. Rūpagosvāmin, the great Chaitanyaite saint, cites from it in his Laghubhāgavatāmṛta15 and the Satsandarbha of Jīvagosvāmin has a number of quotations from it (as in I. 53.; II. 645.; V. 567.; V. 633 etc.).16 Gopālabhaṭṭa,17 one of the six chief Gosvamins of the Chaitanya Order, cites copiously from the first and the second Books of our text in his Haribhaktivilāsa. There

[[Pvi]] are, however, sufficient reasons to believe that the original work suffered interpolations in some places though the number of such passages are insignificant.

Provenance

About the provenance of the work, we agree with Prof. Dinesh Chandra Bhattacharya that it originated somewhere in the Northern part of India though the manuscripts of the work are found mostly in Bengali script, the earliest of which dates 1531 A.D. In his Mathapratiṣṭhātattva18 Raghunandana states “Iti Vallālasenadevāhṛta- dvikhaṇḍākṣara-likhita- Hayaśīrṣapañcarātrīya-samkarṣaṇakande Samudaya-paṭalaḥ”
—and this is a reference to the tradition current in his time regarding the manuscript of the Hayaśīrṣa. We are not sure what the expression ‘dvikhaṇḍākṣara’ exactly means, but the statement leads to the conclusion that the text of the Hayaśīrṣa was not available in Bengal in the 12th century and Vallāla, a zealous patron of learning, was able to procure, evidently with great deal of efforts, a copy of the same and it may be the case that all other manuscripts that appeared in the field subsequently, originated from this very source preserved in the royal library of Vallāla.

Then again, the Hayaśīrṣa (I. 3. 2-15.) appears to be unfavourably disposed not only to such people as the Sivaites, Sauras[19] (though it has a Saurakāṇḍa for the worship of Sūrya), Jains (nagnas) and the heretics, but also to the people hailed from Kāñcī, Koṅkaṇa, Kāverī, Mahārāṣṭra, Kosala, Kāśmīra, Kāmarūpa and Kalinga who are debarred from working as ‘ācārya’ or ‘deśika’ in Pañcarātra rituals. Prof. Bhattacharya thinks that the people of these frontier regions being of predominantly ‘Śaiva’ or ‘Śākta’ inclination are singled out for rejection on purely sectarian grounds. It is, however, interesting to note here that for the transcription of manuscripts the text prescribes the Kāśmīra and Nāgara scripts only20 and no other script finds favour with it (HS., II. 36. 10.).+++(4)+++ This is rather very intriguing.

Date

About the date of the work Prof. Bhattacharya places it [[Pvii]] tentatively in the neighbourhood of the 8th century A.D. The two main grounds of his conclusion are:

(i) In the list of Vaisnava works it refers to a Bhāgavatatantra21 besides several Purāņas and Saṁhitās. It is, however, not certain if this refers to the Bhāgavata as Jīvagosvāmin in his Tattvasandarbha21 refers to Tantrabhāgavata which is to be looked upon as a commentary on the Bhāgavata. But this argument is not sufficiently convincing. Because the word ’tantra’ in this context may have different interpretations.
(ii) In this text, the tirade against the philosophical systems of Jaimini, Buddha, Cārvāka, Jina, Kapila and Gautama (I. 5. 2.) must have a reference to the views amongst others of the great Kumārilabhaṭṭa who refutes the Pañcarātra doctrine in his Tantravārttika. This second ground also may or may not be true, but it is, of course, certain that the Agnipurāṇa is not only acquainted with our text, but actually summarises (Agni, 39, 42.) some of its ‘patalas’.22 Then again, the text gives the features of some of the deities that belong to the earlier periods side by side with some of them belonging to later periods. These lead to the conclusion that the text originated earlier, i.e., nearabout the 6th century A.D. but it continued to grow until it reached its present form sometime in the 7th century or in the neighbourhood of that period.

Apparatus Criticus

The only printed edition of the text available is the First Kanda of the text printed in two parts by Late Pandit Bhuvan Mohan Sankhyatirtha with a Foreword by Prof. Dinesh Chandra Bhattacharya and Introductory Notes by Sri Kshitish Chandra Sarkar, published by the Varendra Research Society, Rajshahi (East Pakistan), in 1953 and 1956. This edition has been referred to as Var, ed. in our edition.

  • A., B., C. — These refer to the manuscripts utilised by the editors of the Varendra Research Society Edition.
  • D. — Refers to the Photocopy of the Paris manuscript No. 848 (Bibl. Nationale), ASB. Ms. No. V.A. 6. (13390). It is a palm-leaf ms. in Bengali script dated 1453 Śakābda, that is 1531 A.D. No. of fols. 126. Contains Adi and Samkarṣana Kandas.
  • E. — Refers to the Calcutta Sanskrit College Manuscript, No.

Narasiṁhavidyāpaṭala separated from the Narasiṁhapratiṣṭhāpaṭala which is actually not a separate Patala. It omits the Kūpavāpīpratiṣṭhāpaṭala.

  • F. — It is also an incomplete and worn out paper manuscript in Bengali script, containing 54 fols. It has the whole of the Ādikāṇḍa and the Samkarṣaṇakanda up-to the Caturmukhasthapanapaṭala. At the end there are two fols. containing different matters. No. Tantra, 158, Sanskrit College, Calcutta.
  • G. — This is Sanskrit College Manuscript No. Tantra, 1549. It contains 37 stray fols. mostly from the Adikāṇḍa. Fols. marked 60 to 90 are, however, intact and consecutive. Other fols. are mutilated and worn out. It seems to have some portions of the ms. F. wrongly separated from the Samkarṣaṇakāṇḍa of the latter. It ends at the end of the Fol. marked 97. Almost all pages are damaged. At the end of it we have a note: “Śricandramādhavanyāyavāgīśasya pustīyam.”
  • H., I., J. — These three mss. have been collated and copied by Sri Haripada Kavyatirtha who kindly prepared for us the press copy of the Samkarṣaṇakāṇḍa. These belong to the private library of Late Bhuvanamohana Samkhyatirtha who in his lifetime kindly left the manuscript at the disposal of Sri Haripada Kavyatirtha for copying for the use of the latter. All these are paper manuscripts written in Bengali script.
  • K. — This is the Adyar Library ms. No. TR 567-1, Vols. I, II. copied by one Jagannatha Mahisura in 1851 A.D. It is complete in all the four Kandas of the work. The transcript copy of the ASB. No. III. H. 444-447 contains 842 pages in all, written in Nagari script. We are indebted to Dr. Samyuktā Gupta for kindly supplying us the call No. of this ms. We gratefully thank her for this help.
  • L. — It is a dated ms. belonging to Pandit Manindra Mohan

readings at the end of the last volume.

This ms. originally belonged to Sri Manomohana Devasarma of the Ishapsahi Pargana and was copied in Sak. 1574 corresponding to 1652 A.D.

After the release of the first Kanda of this work from press, the present editor of the text was able to find out four other manuscripts of the work noted below:

  • 2 manuscripts containing the Samkarṣaṇa-kāṇḍa (Incomplete).
  • 1 manuscript containing the Adikāṇḍa (Incomplete).
  • 1 manuscript containing the Śrīvivāhapaṭala (a chapter of the Samkarsanakanda) (complete).

It is proposed that the results of comparing and study of these four manuscripts will be added as an Appendix at the end of the book.
