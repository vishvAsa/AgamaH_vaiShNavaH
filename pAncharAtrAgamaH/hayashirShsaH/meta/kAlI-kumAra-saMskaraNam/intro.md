FOREWORD 

HAYASİRŞAPANCARĀTRA 

The Asiatic Society undertook the publication of a critical edition of the text Hayasirṣapancharatra, the oldest and the best of the literary documents of the Pancharatra sect of the Vaisnavas. Besides its religious bearings, the text furnishes extremely significant details relating to iconographical features of deities and architectural norms of temple con- struction and reconstruction. Several manuscripts of the text have been traced, and the task of collating and editing them for the use of the scholars was assigned to Prof. K. K. Dutta Sastri and the work of writing iconographical notes to Dr. K. K. Das Gupta. Due to some unavoidable. reasons the publication of the volume took several years and we are sorry for it. Both Prof. K. K. Dutta Sastri and Dr. K. K. Das Gupta did their best in editing the text and compiling iconographical notes. The Society is thankful to them for their untiring efforts and interests shown in the publication of this important Vaisnava text. 

We believe, the scholars at home and abroad will find the text extremely useful in their academic pursuits, more particularly in the fields of religion, iconography and architecture.

INTRODUCTION 

The *Pañcaratra System*: The Pañcarātra is a sect of the Vaisnavas the earliest reference to which may be found in the *Satapatha Brāhmaṇa* (xiii. 6\. 1\.) in which the *'puruṣa Nārāyaṇa*' is said to have conceived the idea of *'pañcaratra satra*' or a 'sacrificial rite to be continued for five days. In the 'pancaratra' circle, however, the word 'ratra' came to be interpreted differently. The apocryphal Nāradīya takes the word 'ratra' to mean 'knowledge' and speaks of five kinds of 'rātras', namely, Tattva (Ontology or Cosmology), Muktiprada (Liberation), Bhakti- prada (Devotion), Yaugika (Yoga or meditation) and Vaiseṣika (the objects of senses). Our text seems to represent a different school of thought according to which the word 'ratra' means 'night' which is equated with the five Mahābhūtas and which holds that the Supreme Being is above all the qualities (nirañjana), and being known causes salvation by dint of which man becomes identical with Visņu himself. The text, therefore, that imparts this knowledge of the Supreme Being and helps to distiguish the 'nights' from the Ultimate Reality is known as Pañcarātra (Hayaśīrṣa, I. 4\. 1\.\-3). The Agnipurāṇa, a Puranic text of the Phagavats of the Pañcarātra sect, however, speaks of a 'saptarātra' also but the term has not been explained there (Agni, 39\. 1.) 

In the *Mahabharata2* (XII. 338\. 4.) the word 'pāñcarātrika' is used as an epithet of Visnu and Pañcaratra also occurs in XII. 349\. 1\. The *Harṣacarita* of Bana (7th cen.) names in its Ucchvāsa, VII the Fañcarātras along with the Bhagavatas. The '*caturvyuha*' or the quadruple emanations of Visnu, namely, Kṛṣṇa, Vasudeva or Nārāyaṇa,

*Literature* of the *school*: The ancient literature of the Pañcarātra school appears to be a vast one and Schrader10 gives a list of about 215 texts on the Pañcarătra. Our text (I. 2\. 2-6.) gives a list of 25 authoritative texts of the Pañcarātra along with an account of some other texts that are common to all of the Bhāgavata sects (I. 2\. 8-9.). Agnipurāņa says ditto to the list and the *Mantrakaumudi* of the celeblated Maithila scholar Devanatha Tarkapañcānana (16th cen.) reproduces the same. The Hayasirṣa is held to be the 'adya' (the best or the earliest) of ail these texts. The original text is said to have been narrated by Brahmā to Mahavara and the sage Mārkaṇḍeya entreated by Bhrgu reproduces the old text to him in the form of the present text. Yamunācārya11 informs us that, according to the Mahābhārata and the Skandapurāṇa, Kesava is the 'vakta' or the first narrator of the Pañcarātra. Possibly this relates to the school and not the text.

The diction is generally graceful and technical terms are freely used specially in connection with scuptural and architectural matters, but these terms are conventional. The text is complete in *four* books (*Kāṇḍas*) and they are: Ādi, *Samkarṣaṇa*, Lainga and *Saura*. Each of these Kandas are divided into several sections named '*patalas*'. The whole work is said to be a collection of 12,000 ('dvādaśasāhasrika') 'granthas' and possibly not of the verses as supposed by some scholars. A grantha, (also called a 'sloka') according to the scribal tradition, is made of 32 syllables and the text available to us contains approximately 11,538 such granthas. If, however, the expression 'dvādaśasāhasrika' is used with reference to the verses, then we must have to presume that almost half of the text is now lost to us. 

The text describes itself as a 'pratiṣṭhātantra' (I. 1\. 28.; I. 3\. 14.) and mainly deals with such topics as the details of Architecture in relation to construction and reconstruction of temples, assembly halls and such other structures, mostly in conformity with the Northern school, installation and construction of images, copying and donation of manuscripts, erection of banners, dedication of wells and tanks, salvage of wrecked ones and various other topics connected with all these things. Philosophical topics came up only incidentally and these are dealt with in a succinct manner. The text is looked upon as an authoritative work on 'smrti' and the Vaisnavas of Bengal appear to have a special fascina- tion for it. *Halayudha* and *Raghunandana*, the great smrti scholar of Bengal (16th cen.) in his *Mathaphatisthatattva12* cite our text in connection with the measurement of the flag*\-*pole etc. *Rupagosvāmin,* the great Chaitanyaite saint, cites from it in his *Laghubhāgavatāmṛta13* and the *Satsandarbha* of *Jivagosvāmin* has a number of quotations *from* it (*as* in I. 53.; II. 645.; V. 567.; V. 633 etc.).\* *Gopālabhaṭṭa*," one of the six chief Gosvamins of the Chaitanya Order, cites copiously from the first and the second Books of our text in his *Haribhaktivilāsa*. *Provenance*: About the provenance of the work, we agree with Prof. Dinesh Chandra Bhattacharya that it originated somewhere in the Northern part of India though the manuscripts of the work are found mostly in Bengali script, the earliest of which dates 1531 A.D. In his Mathapratiṣṭhātattva15 Raghunandana states "Iti Vallālasenadevāhṛta- dvikhaṇḍākṣaralikhita\-Hayaśīrṣapañcarātrīyasamkarṣaṇakande Samudaya- paṭalah"\--and this is a reference to the tradition current in his time regarding the manuscript of the Hayaśīrşa. We are not sure what the expression 'dvikhaṇḍākṣara' exactly means, but the statement leads to the conclusion that the text of the Hayasirșa was not available in Bengal in the 12th century and *Vallāla*, a zealous patron of learning, was able to procure, evidently with great deal of efforts, a copy of the same and it may be the case that all other manuscripts that appeared in the field subsequently, originated from this very source preserved in the royal library of Vallāla. Then again, the Hayaśīrṣa (I. 3\. 2-15.) appears to be unfavourably disposed not only to such people as the Sivaites, Sauras1o (though it has a Saurakāṇḍa for the worship of Sūrya), Jains (nagnas) and the heretics, but also to the people hailed from Kāñcī, Końkaṇa, Kāverī, Mahārāṣṭra, Kosala, Kāśmīra, Kāmarūpa and Kalinga who are debarred from working as 'ācārya' or 'deśika' in Pañcarātra rituals. Prof. Bhattacharya thinks that the people of these frontier regions being of predominantly 'Saiva' or ‘Šākta' inclination are singled out for rejection on purely sectarian grounds. It is, however, interesting to note here that for the transcription of manuscripts the text prescribes the Kaśmīra and Nāgara scripts only17 and no other script finds favour with it (HS., II. 36\. 10\.). This is rather very intriguing.
