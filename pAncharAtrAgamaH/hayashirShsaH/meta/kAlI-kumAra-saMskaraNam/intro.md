## The Pañcarātra System
The Pañcarātra is a sect of the Vaiṣṇavas the earliest reference to which may be found in the *Satapatha Brāhmaṇa* (xiii. 6. 1.)[^i_1] in which the *'puruṣa Nārāyaṇa'* is said to have conceived the idea of *'pañcarātra satra'* or a 'sacrificial rite to be continued for five days.' In the 'pañcarātra' circle, however, the word 'rātra' came to be interpreted differently. The apocryphal Nāradīya takes the word 'rātra' to mean 'knowledge' and speaks of five kinds of 'rātras', namely, Tattva (Ontology or Cosmology), Muktiprada (Liberation), Bhaktiprada (Devotion), Yaugika (Yoga or meditation) and Vaiseṣika (the objects of senses). Our text seems to represent a different school of thought according to which the word 'rātra' means 'night' which is equated with the five Mahābhūtas and which holds that the Supreme Being is above all the qualities (nirañjana), and being known causes salvation by dint of which man becomes identical with Viṣṇu himself. The text, therefore, that imparts this knowledge of the Supreme Being and helps to distinguish the 'nights' from the Ultimate Reality is known as Pañcarātra (*Hayaśīrṣa*, I. 4. 1.-3). The *Agnipurāṇa*, a Puranic text of the Bhāgavatas of the Pañcarātra sect, however, speaks of a 'saptarātra' also but the term has not been explained there (*Agni*, 39. 1.)

In the *Mahābhārata*[^i_2] (XII. 338. 4.) the word 'pāñcarātrika' is used as an epithet of Viṣṇu and Pañcarātra also occurs in XII. 349. 1. The *Harṣacarita* of Bana (7th cen.) names in its Ucchvāsa VII the Pañcarātras along with the Bhagavatas. The '*caturvyūha*'[^i_asterisk] or the quadruple emanations of Viṣṇu, namely, Kṛṣṇa, Vasudeva or Nārāyaṇa,

## Literature
***Literature* of the *school*:** The ancient literature of the Pañcarātra school appears to be a vast one and Schrader[^iv_10] gives a list of about 215 texts on the Pañcarātra. Our text (I. 2. 2-6.) gives a list of 25 authoritative texts of the Pañcarātra along with an account of some other texts that are common to all of the Bhāgavata sects (I. 2. 8-9.). Agnipurāṇa says ditto to the list and the *Mantrakaumudī* of the celebrated Maithila scholar Devanatha Tarkapañcānana (16th cen.) reproduces the same. The Hayaśīrṣa is held to be the 'ādya' (the best or the earliest) of all these texts. The original text is said to have been narrated by Brahmā to Mahāvara and the sage Mārkaṇḍeya entreated by Bhṛgu reproduces the old text to him in the form of the present text. Yāmunācārya[^iv_11] informs us that, according to the *Mahābhārata* and the *Skandapurāṇa*,[^iv_double_dagger] Keśava is the 'vaktā' or the first narrator of the Pañcarātra. Possibly this relates to the school and not the text.

## Nature, contents and extent
The Hayaśīrṣa Pañcarātra is written thoroughly in the Puranic style and language and includes as such a number of un-Paninian words in the fashion of the Puranas, a list of which we propose to append at the end of the last volume. The whole work is written in the verses of 'anuṣṭubh' metre interspersed with some 'mantra' portions in prose. The diction is generally graceful and technical terms are freely used specially in connection with sculptural and architectural matters, but these terms are conventional. The text is complete in *four* books (*Kāṇḍas*) and they are: Ādi, *Samkarṣaṇa*, Lainga and *Saura*. Each of these Kandas are divided into several sections named '*patalas*'. The whole work is said to be a collection of 12,000 ('dvādaśasāhasrika') 'granthas' and possibly not of the verses as supposed by some scholars. A grantha (also called a 'śloka'), according to the scribal tradition, is made of 32 syllables and the text available to us contains approximately 11,538 such granthas. If, however, the expression 'dvādaśasāhasrika' is used with reference to the verses, then we must have to presume that almost half of the text is now lost to us.

The text describes itself as a 'pratiṣṭhātantra' (I. 1. 28.; I. 3. 14.) and mainly deals with such topics as the details of Architecture in relation to construction and reconstruction of temples, assembly halls and such other structures, mostly in conformity with the Northern school, installation and construction of images, copying and donation of manuscripts, erection of banners, dedication of wells and tanks, salvage of wrecked ones and various other topics connected with all these things. Philosophical topics came up only incidentally and these are dealt with in a succinct manner.+++(5)+++ 

The text is looked upon as an authoritative work on 'smṛti' and the Vaisnavas of Bengal appear to have a special fascination for it. *Halāyudha* and *Raghunandana*, the great smṛti scholar of Bengal (16th cen.) in his *Mathapratiṣṭhātattva*[^iv_12] cite our text in connection with the measurement of the flag-pole etc. *Rūpagosvāmin,* the great Chaitanyaite saint, cites from it in his *Laghubhāgavatāmṛta*[^iv_13] and the *Satsandarbha* of *Jīvagosvāmin* has a number of quotations *from* it (*as* in I. 53.; II. 645.; V. 567.; V. 633 etc.).[^iv_asterisk] *Gopālabhaṭṭa*,[^iv_14] one of the six chief Gosvamins of the Chaitanya Order, cites copiously from the first and the second Books of our text in his *Haribhaktivilāsa*. ## Provenance 
About the provenance of the work, we agree with Prof. Dinesh Chandra Bhattacharya that it originated somewhere in the Northern part of India though the manuscripts of the work are found mostly in Bengali script, the earliest of which dates 1531 A.D. In his *Mathapratiṣṭhātattva*[^vi_15] Raghunandana states "Iti Vallālasenadevāhṛta- dvikhaṇḍākṣara-likhita- Hayaśīrṣapañcarātrīya-samkarṣaṇakande Samudaya-paṭalaḥ"  
—and this is a reference to the tradition current in his time regarding the manuscript of the Hayaśīrṣa. We are not sure what the expression 'dvikhaṇḍākṣara' exactly means, but the statement leads to the conclusion that the text of the Hayaśīrṣa was not available in Bengal in the 12th century and *Vallāla*, a zealous patron of learning, was able to procure, evidently with great deal of efforts, a copy of the same and it may be the case that all other manuscripts that appeared in the field subsequently, originated from this very source preserved in the royal library of Vallāla. 

Then again, the Hayaśīrṣa (I. 3. 2-15.) appears to be unfavourably disposed not only to such people as the Sivaites, Sauras[[^vi_16]] (though it has a Saurakāṇḍa for the worship of Sūrya), Jains (nagnas) and the heretics, but also to the people hailed from Kāñcī, Koṅkaṇa, Kāverī, Mahārāṣṭra, Kosala, Kāśmīra, Kāmarūpa and Kalinga who are debarred from working as 'ācārya' or 'deśika' in Pañcarātra rituals. Prof. Bhattacharya thinks that the people of these frontier regions being of predominantly 'Śaiva' or ‘Śākta' inclination are singled out for rejection on purely sectarian grounds. It is, however, interesting to note here that for the transcription of manuscripts the text prescribes the Kāśmīra and Nāgara scripts only[^vi_17] and no other script finds favour with it (HS., II. 36. 10.).+++(4)+++ This is rather very intriguing.

## Date
About the date of the work Prof. Bhattacharya places it 
[[Pvii]] tentatively in the neighbourhood of the 8th century A.D. The two main grounds of his conclusion are: 

(i) In the list of Vaisnava works it refers to a *Bhāgavatatantra*[^vii_18] besides several Purāņas and Saṁhitās. It is, however, not certain if this refers to the *Bhāgavata* as *Jīvagosvāmin* in his *Tattvasandarbha*[^vii_18] refers to *Tantrabhāgavata* which is to be looked upon as a commentary on the *Bhāgavata*. But this argument is not sufficiently convincing. Because the word 'tantra' in this context may have different interpretations.  
(ii) In this text, the tirade against the philosophical systems of Jaimini, Buddha, Cārvāka, Jina, Kapila and Gautama (I. 5. 2.) must have a reference to the views amongst others of the great *Kumārilabhaṭṭa* who refutes the Pañcarātra doctrine in his *Tantravārttika*. This second ground also may or may not be true, but it is, of course, certain that the *Agnipurāṇa* is not only acquainted with our text, but actually summarises (*Agni*, 39, 42.) some of its 'patalas'.[^vii_19] Then again, the text gives the features of some of the deities that belong to the earlier periods side by side with some of them belonging to later periods. These lead to the conclusion that the text originated earlier, i.e., nearabout the 6th century A.D. but it continued to grow until it reached its present form sometime in the 7th century or in the neighbourhood of that period.
