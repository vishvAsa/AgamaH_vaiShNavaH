The *Hayasirṣa Pañcarātra* is rich in iconographical materials. Indeed, such materials can be profitably compared and correlated with similar data in other texts, such as, the *Brhat\-Samhita, Caturvarga*\-*cintamani, Rūpamaṇḍana, Mānasāra* and *Silparatna*, apart from the notable Purāņas like the Agni, *Matsya*, *Märkandeya* and *Visnudharmottara*. This com- parative study will not only enable us to trace the development of concept and iconography of several Brahmanical divinities described in them, but may also throw some light on the stratification of the Puranic literature. Presently, however, we are confined to the iconographic data culled *from* our text and have endeavoured to find out a correspondence between these materials and actual representations of the relevant gods and goddesses. 

Of the five principal Brahmanical divinities the Ādi Kāṇḍa of the *Hayasirṣa Pañcarātra* expatiates on the iconography of Visņu, Śiva and Devi. The *Saura Kāṇḍa* has a complete chapter on the images of Surya, and there is a separate chapter on the iconography of Ganapati in the *Samkarṣaṇa Kāṇḍa* of our text. Of the other deities the *Hayaśīrṣa* speaks about Brahmă, Grahas (Planets), Lokeśas (guardians of different quarters) and Garuḍa.\* It also describes different varieties of the phallic emblem of Siva and its constituent parts. Not unexpectedly, the text dilates on the methods of the installation of images in temples along with the materials for fashioning divine images and various rituals to be passed through from start to finish for manufacturing and installing them. The relevant prescriptions are more or less identical with those found in other texts. As regards proper iconographic materials the Chapters of the *Adi* Kāṇḍa numbering 1, 18 and 20-30 are valuable and hence discussed 

below.

Plastic representations of Hayagrīva, though rare, have been met with. There are three images of the four\-handed variety of the deity in the Kotah Museum, Rajasthan. Of about 10-11th century, all of them are of the *sthanaka* variety and are identical, in respect of style, attributes and ornaments, except the fact that one of them (exhibit no. 262) shows a serpent, in place of the usual *pustaka*; the presence of serpent in this specimen is not unusual in view of the fact that in the *Visnudharmottaram* (III. 7\) Hayagrīva has been described as a form of Samkarṣaṇa, who in turn is a form of Ananta\-naga, the king of the sankes; the other cognisances of the god in these statues are *gadā*, *kamaṇḍalu* and *varada\-mudra*. Another good specimen is en- countered on a niche of the circumambulatory passage of the Lakṣmaṇa temple at Khajuraho. Examples of the seated type are furnished by two images of the Hayagriva shrine at Tiruvendipuram (once a noted centre of the Hayagrīva worship) near Cuddalore, Madras. Chapter 18 of our text, entitled *Pratimā\-lakṣmaṇa* (*devatārccānu- kirttana* of some Purāņas), deals more with iconometry than with iconography. In other words, it lays down certain rules regarding measurements for making images of Vasudeva and his consorts. Such details are found in several slokas (7-50). As regards the representation of Vasudeva, it is stated in this chapter that the god should be shown as four\-handed with *cakra* and *gadā* in the upper right and left hands and *padma* and *sankha* in the lower right and left hands respectively. Images of his consorts, Śrī and Puști, should go up to the height of his thigh and should have slight flexions in their postures. Śrī has to carry a *padma* and Puṣṭi a vīņā. The *prabhāmaṇḍala* of the image is to show the effigies of the garland\-bearing Vidyādharas and Gajavyālas and the pedestal *is* to be of the shape of a lotus. The chapter concludes with the suggestion that the image may be made in accordance with the local customs, in other words, local taste and outlook. Images of Visnu, tallying with the description set forth above, have been found in different parts of Northern India, since as is well known, relevant icons. from the South replace Puşți by Bhū\-devi. Further, the prescriptions regarding the presence of Vidyādharas and Gajavyālas in the image concerned tend to point to a late date when the iconic type was fully developed.

The 21st Chapter gives an account of Garuda, the vehicle of Viṣņu. Son of Vinata (hence his name *Vainateya,* which appears as the title of the present Chapter) and Kasyapa, Garuda has been described in our text as follows: he is handsome in appearance, possesses a human body and a pointed nose resembling the beak of a bird; he has curly hair, roundish eyes, a pair of lustrous wings; his legs are adorned with tinkling bells made of snakes and the nails of his legs are bedecked with the coils of snakes; he wears keyūras of lotuses, ear\-rings made of scorpions and a *kulika* in waist; his image is to be placed either on a pillar or within a *manḍapa*;2 in the first case the height of the pillar should be half the breadth of the temple in whose front the pillar is to be set up, while in the latter case the *maṇḍapa* is to be made in accordance of the measurement of the temple (of Vasudeva) standing in its front. 

This description, though short, brings out the essential character- istics of Garuda, including his close association with serpents. The half\-bird half\-human vehicle of Visnu is known in literature under as many *as* five names, Satya, Suparṇa, Garuḍa, Tarkṣya and Vihageśvara (same as Avatara Vihangama of the lists of the *Ahirbudhnya* and *Sattvata* Samhitas*)*, of which the first four are associated in a text with the four directions; in other words, images of Garuda found on the four corners of a typical Vaisnava temple are known by these four names. The 22nd Chapter of the Hayasirṣa *Pañcarātra* delineates the vyūha\* (emanatory) aspect of the primeval god Vasudeva. According to the Pañcarātra philosophy, Vişņu expresses himself in four vyūhas (caturvyūha), known as Vasudeva, Samkarṣana, Pradyumna and Aniruddha. In course of time the number of vyūhas rose up to twenty- four (caturvimsati\-vyuha). The Pañcaratra literature enjoins that the Supreme lord Vasudeva creates Samkarṣaṇa, Samkarṣaṇa in his turn Pradyumna and the latter in his turn Aniruddha; from each of them descends three sub\-vyūhas (vyūhāntaras) and to these twelve sub\-vyūhas is added again a set of twelve forms, called in some texts Vidyesvaras (see tables B and C). The process by which the vyūhas are said to emanate is as follows: from Vasudeva emanates Keśava, Nārāyaṇa and Madhava; from Samkarṣaṇa Govinda, Viṣṇu and Madhusudana; from Pradyumna Trivikrama, Vamana and Śrīdhara; and from Aniruddha Hṛṣikeśa, Padmanabha and Damodara; again from para\-Vaasudeva appears another Vāsudeva (vyūha\-Vasudeva), from the latter Purusottama and from him Janardana; from Samkarṣaṇa another Samkarṣaṇa, from him Adhokṣaja, and from the latter Upendra; from Pradyumna another Pradyumna, from him Nrisimha and from the latter Hari; and then from Aniruddha a second Aniruddha, from him Acyuta and from the latter Śrī\-Kṛṣṇa. Arithmetically, the permutations of four attributes of Visnu admit of his twenty\-four forms. What is important to take note of is that whether in the case of caturmurti or in that of caturvimsati- mūrti, the principle of monotheism was never lost sight of. This is also illustrated by the icons of both the groups, i.e., caturmurtis and caturvimsati\-murtis**.**

Plastically, the caturvyūhas are combined into one iconic type. The composite image, known as Vaikuntha, shows the lord with four faces and four hands (and rarely more than four arms). Such images mostly hail from Kashmir, the region where the vyūha aspect of Viṣņu seems to have earned popularity (cf. *caturātmā Kesava* as mentioned by the Kashmirian author Kalhana in his *Rajatarangini)*. In these icons the central face is human, side faces on the right and left are of a lion and a boar, and the back face is of an ugly demon; usually the deity is seen holding a lotus and a conch\-shell in the front right and left hands respectively and placing his back hands on the personified weapons like Cakrapuruşa and Gadādevī. While the Martanda temple specimens being relievo representations are three\-faced, statues coming from Avantipura, which are in the round, are four\-faced. An image from the Martanda temple shows the god with eight hands, most of the hands being, how- ever, broken. An advancement on the usual iconographic form of the caturmurti, as illustrated by these Kashmir images, can be seen in the increase **of** the number of hands and sometimes of heads as well. An 11th\-century image, now in the Ajmer Museum, showing Visņu with as many *as* 14 hands, of which five only survive, is a representative example; another interesting feature of this sculpture lies in the presence of Garuda*,* who is seen respectfully carrying his master. The Garuḍāsana caturmurti (described as 'Vaikuntheśvara' in a contemporary inscrip- tion) enshrined in the main sanctum of Lakṣmaṇa temple at Khajuraho is characterised by eight figures (another unusual device) carved on either side of the outer aura of the main image; probably this cluster of figures represent eight of the Kesavādimurtis and the main image symbolize the four primary ones, viz., Vasudeva, Samkarṣaṇa, Pradyumna and Aniruddha.

The twenty\-four forms of the god, as represented in art, are all standing figures, possessing four arms and wearing the *kiriṭamukuța* and other usual ornaments; the difference between each of them lies only in the order of the attributes*\-*sankha*, cakra, gadā* and *padma*\-held by the four hands of the deity; the order of these objects is in passing, *as* in a circle, from the upper right hand to the upper left hand, thence to the lower left hand and lastly to the lower right hand. Thus the image of the deity showing *sankha, chakra*, *gadā* and *padma* in the hands in the order mentioned above, beginning from the upper right and ending with the lower hight hand, represents the form styled 'Keśava'. The twelve forms of Visnu beginning with Kesava and the order in which the emblems are held in the four hands of the deity, as described in our text, are given in the Tables appended. The *Hayasirṣa* list may be compared with those of the *Rūpamaṇḍana* and the *Padma-purāṇa*. It may be noted here that in our text the disposition of attributes in the hands of Kesava (no. 1\) is identical with that of Damodara (no. 12), and arrangement of the said emblems in the hands of Nārāyaṇa (no. 2\) is exactly similar to that in the hands of Padmanābha (no. 11\). Similar errors are also noticeable in the *Padma\-purāņa*. In fact, the *Rupamaṇḍana* list seems to be more correct than those of many other texts. 

Barring the Hoysala temples of Mysore of the 12th\-13th century, which depict most of these twenty\-four forms of the god on their walls, they are not found together forming a single\-group in early Indian repertory. A 17th\-century Nepalese painting, now in the Prince of Wales Museum, Bombay, bears, however, representations of all of them. Stray images of one or other forms have, however, been discovered throughout the country and are preserved in different museums. Among them Trivikrama is commonly found in Northern India. Generally, caturviṁśati- murtis are of the *sthānaka* order, but *āsana* examples, though rare, are also not unknown. Thus the Bangiya Sahitya Parisat Museum, Calcutta, possesses a unique bronze image of Sridhara (Hṛśikeșa according to the *Padma\-purāṇa*) showing the deity with *padma*, *cakra,* gadā and *śańkha* in the lower right, upper right, upper left and lower left hands of the deity respectively; it is also interesting to note here that with the exception of the first, *all* the other emblems are *placed* on full\-blown lotuses, springing from stalks held by the god. And further, this statue is in agreement with the description of the *Hayaŝirṣa*, according to which, Sridhara may be alternatively depicted as seated.
