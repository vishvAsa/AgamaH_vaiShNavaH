ICONOGRAPHICAL NOTES

**By**

DR. KALYAN KUMAR DASGUPTA

## §1 ICONOGRAPHICAL NOTES

The *[[Hayaśīrṣa|Hayaśīrṣa]] Pañcarātra* is rich in iconographical materials. Indeed, such materials can be profitably compared and correlated with similar data in other texts, such as, the *Bṛhat-Saṁhitā, Caturvarga*-*cintāmaṇi, Rūpamaṇḍana, Mānasāra* and *Śilparatna*, apart from the notable Purāṇas like the Agni, *Matsya*, *[[Märkandeya|Mārkaṇḍeya]]* and *Viṣṇudharmottara*. This [[com- parative|comparative]] study will not only enable us to trace the development of concept and iconography of several Brahmanical [[divini- ties|divinities]] described in them, but may also throw some light on the stratification of the Puranic literature. Presently, however, we are confined to the iconographic data culled from our text and have endeavoured to find out a correspondence between these materials and actual representations of the relevant gods and goddesses.

Of the five principal Brahmanical divinities, the Ādi Kāṇḍa of the *[[Hayaśīrṣa|Hayaśīrṣa]] Pañcarātra* expatiates on the iconography of Viṣṇu, Śiva and Devī. The *Saura Kāṇḍa* has a complete chapter on the images of Sūrya, and there is a separate chapter on the iconography of Gaṇapati in the *Saṁkarṣaṇa Kāṇḍa* of our text. Of the other deities, the *Hayaśīrṣa* speaks about Brahmā, Grahas (Planets), Lokeśas (guardians of different quarters) and Garuḍa.[^141_asterisk] It also describes different varieties of the phallic emblem of Śiva and its constituent parts. Not unexpectedly, the text dilates on the methods of the installation of images in temples along with the materials for fashioning divine images and various rituals to be passed through from start to finish for manufacturing and installing them. The relevant prescriptions are more or less identical with those found in other texts. As regards proper iconographic materials, the Chapters of the *Ādi* Kāṇḍa numbering 1, 18 and 20–30 are valuable and hence discussed below.

Chapter 1 of our text describes [[Hayaśīrṣa|Hayaśīrṣa]] or Hayagrīva ('horse-faced'), an incarnatory form of Viṣṇu. According to the Vaiṣṇava mythology, the lord in this avatāra form got back for Brahmā the Vedas seized by the demons Madhu and Kaiṭabha (*Mahābhārata,* XII. 335. 22–72). The name 'Hayagrīva' has also been applied to a demon who was killed by Viṣṇu after assuming the horse-headed form. The well-known story of the slaying of the horse-demon, Keśin, by Kṛṣṇa may also be recalled here. The *Brahmāṇḍa-purāṇa* (IV. 5. 9; 35–40) states that Hayagrīva-Viṣṇu appeared before Agastya at Kāñcī with *śaṅkha, cakra, akṣavalaya* and *pustaka* in his four hands.

The present text (Śls. 21, 23), like most others, describes Hayagrīva-Viṣṇu as [[horse- faced1|horse-faced]][^142_1] and four-armed; he holds *śaṅkha, cakra, gadā* and *śārṅga.* In Ch. 25 (vv. 22–25), however, he is described as holding *śaṅkha*, *cakra, gadā* and Veda. In other texts, he has variously been described as multi-handed; when he is eight- or twelve-handed, he may show [[addi- tional|additional]] emblems like *padma*, *akṣamālā, vara*, *pustaka*, etc. Whether he is to be shown in the standing or seated posture, nothing definite has been said in our text.

Plastic representations of Hayagrīva, though rare, have been met with. There are three images of the four-handed variety of the deity in the Kotah Museum, Rajasthan. Of about 10–11th century, all of them are of the *sthānaka* variety and are identical, in respect of style, attributes and ornaments, except the fact that one of them (exhibit no. 262) shows a serpent, in place of the usual *pustaka*; the presence of serpent in this specimen is not unusual in view of the fact that in the *Viṣṇudharmottara* (III. 7) Hayagrīva has been described as a form of Saṁkarṣaṇa, who in turn is a form of Ananta-nāga, the king of the [[sankes|snakes]]; the other cognisances of the god in these statues are *gadā*, *kamaṇḍalu* and *varada-mudrā*. Another good specimen is [[en- countered|encountered]] on a niche of the circumambulatory passage of the Lakṣmaṇa temple at Khajuraho. Examples of the seated type are furnished by two images of the Hayagrīva shrine at Tiruvendipuram (once a noted centre of the Hayagrīva worship) near Cuddalore, Madras. The first of them shows the god seated in yogic posture with crossed legs, a *yogapaṭṭa* binding them; of his four hands, the normal ones are placed on the knees, while the back right and left hands carry a *cakra* and a *śaṅkha* respectively; in the second image the deity is accompanied by his consort, Lakṣmī, the latter sitting on his left lap with a bud in her left hand; of his four hands, the upper pair carries a *cakra* and a *śaṅkha*, [[P143]] the lower right displays the *vyākhyāna*-*mudrā* (the slightly open mouth, indicative of speaking, fits well with this *mudrā*) and the lower left goes round the waist of his consort. A representative specimen of eight-handed form of Hayagrīva can be seen in the temple of Nuggehalli (Karṇāṭaka).

With some orientations, Hayagrīva entered the Buddhist pantheon and his images have been discovered in Tibet, China and Japan (see Van Gulik's *Hayagrīva*). Vidyārāja Hayagrīva, an aspect of Avalokiteśvara of the Mahāyāna Buddhism, may have been inspired by the concept of Hayagrīva of the Vaiṣṇava cult where he is the rescuer of the Vedas and hence the bestower of knowledge.

Chapter 18 of our text, entitled *Pratimā-lakṣaṇa* (*devatārccānukīrttana* of some Purāṇas), deals more with iconometry than with iconography. In other words, it lays down certain rules regarding measurements for making images of Vāsudeva and his consorts. Such details are found in several ślokas (7–50). As regards the representation of Vāsudeva, it is stated in this chapter that the god should be shown as four-handed with *cakra* and *gadā* in the upper right and left hands and *padma* and *śaṅkha* in the lower right and left hands respectively. Images of his consorts, Śrī and Puṣṭi, should go up to the height of his thigh and should have slight flexions in their postures. Śrī has to carry a *padma* and Puṣṭi a vīṇā. The *prabhāmaṇḍala* of the image is to show the effigies of the garland-bearing Vidyādharas and Gajavyālas and the pedestal is to be of the shape of a lotus. The chapter concludes with the suggestion that the image may be made in accordance with the local customs, in other words, local taste and outlook. Images of Viṣṇu, tallying with the description set forth above, have been found in different parts of Northern India, since as is well known, relevant icons from the South replace Puṣṭi by Bhū-devī. Further, the prescriptions regarding the presence of Vidyādharas and Gajavyālas in the image concerned tend to point to a late date when the iconic type was fully developed.

Chapter 20 deals with the iconography of Śrī, i.e., Śrī-Lakṣmī, apparently as an independent divinity. It has been laid down in this Chapter that the image of the goddess is to be of *aṣṭatāla* (i.e., 8 × 12 = 96 *aṅgulas*) *type*; it should portray Lakṣmī as an ideal of feminine beauty, as it were, [[char- acterised|characterised]] by fully rounded breasts, a narrow waist and heavy buttocks and as holding a lotus and a *śrīphala* (the fruit of the [[P144]] [[bilva|bilva]] tree, Latin *Aegle marmelos*) in her right and left hands respectively. It is further stated that the goddess is to be bathed by two elephants by waters poured from two pitchers and she is to be attended by two chowrie-bearers on two sides. Iconographically, the type just described is known as Gaja-Lakṣmī and some of the earliest representations of Gaja-Lakṣmī are met with on coins (e.g., an uninscribed coin from Kauśāmbī of 3rd century B.C.; coins of Viśākhadeva, Śivadatta and probably also of Vāyudeva of Ayodhyā of circa 1st century B.C., monetary issues of alien rulers like Azilises, Rājuvula and Śoḍāsa of 1st century B.C.–1st century A.D.).

The 21st Chapter gives an account of Garuḍa, the vehicle of Viṣṇu. Son of Vinatā (hence his name *Vainateya,* which appears as the title of the present Chapter) and Kaśyapa, Garuḍa has been described in our text as follows: he is handsome in appearance, possesses a human body and a pointed nose resembling the beak of a bird; he has curly hair, roundish eyes, a pair of lustrous wings; his legs are adorned with tinkling bells made of snakes and the nails of his legs are bedecked with the coils of snakes; he wears keyūras of lotuses, ear-rings made of scorpions and a *kulika* in waist; his image is to be placed either on a pillar or within a [[maṇḍapa;2|maṇḍapa;]][^144_asterisk] in the first case the height of the pillar should be half the breadth of the temple in whose front the pillar is to be set up, while in the latter case the *maṇḍapa* is to be made in accordance of the measurement of the temple (of Vāsudeva) standing in its front.

This description, though short, brings out the essential [[charac- teristics|characteristics]] of Garuḍa, including his close association with serpents. The half-bird half-human vehicle of Viṣṇu is known in literature under as many as five names: Satya, Suparṇa, Garuḍa, Tārkṣya and Vihageśvara (same as Avatāra Vihangama of the lists of the *Ahirbudhnya* and *Sāttvata* Saṁhitās), of which the first four are associated in a text with the four directions; in other words, images of Garuḍa found on the four corners of a typical Vaiṣṇava temple are known by these four names. As regards the arms of Garuḍa, the number varies from [[two-armed|two]][^144_3] to sixteen. His usual *mudrā* is *namaskāra-mudrā* and his attributes include *śaṅkha*, *cakra, gadā*, *śārṅga, kamaṇḍalu,* khaḍga, *aṅkuśa*, *muṣala* and *sarpa*. The *Hayaśīrṣa,* however, does not say anything about the attribute or the *mudrā* of the god.

[^144_asterisk]: \* The reading *maṇḍapa* of the mss. B and C accords with reality and hence accepted by me.
[^144_3]: 3 I prefer the reading *dvibhujau* to *dvimukhau*, though the latter expression may [[P145]] also be considered valid in view of the sculptures serving as pillar capitals where Garuḍa appears as Janiform, his whole figure (not face alone) being double-sided and facing opposite ways; in these specimens he is endowed with two arms exhibiting the *namaskāramudrā* and the usual bird-like features, such as, the beak, wings and claws.

In the earliest phase of the iconography of Garuḍa, he appears as a parrot-like bird; this is illustrated by a similar figure with ear-rings and a bushy tuft carved on the inner side of the middle architrave of the eastern gateway of Sanchi (1st century B.C.). In the art of Gandhāra of the 1st–2nd century A.D., he is portrayed as a well-decorated eagle usually carrying up to the sky a Nāga and a Nāginī either with his long beak or his big talons. In the Gupta numismatic art of the 4th–5th century A.D., Garuḍa is depicted as a full-fledged bird with impressive wings, surmounting a column (*Garuḍadhvaja*); he is sometimes seen holding a snake in his mouth, a feature reminiscent of his epithet *pannagāśana* occurring in literature. The repertory of the early mediaeval period shows Garuḍa either as the *vāhana* of Viṣṇu or as the capital piece of a column or a sculpture in the round in front of a Vaiṣṇava temple. Such figures of the man-bird attendant of Viṣṇu, answering amply to the above-noted description, usually portrays him as kneeling on the right knee and with the hands disposed in *namaskāra-mudrā*. Normally he is two-armed, but four-handed specimens are also not unknown. In a Viṣṇu sculpture of the early mediaeval period, found near Laksmankati in Bangladesh, Garuḍa is seen carrying his Master on his back; of his four hands, the back ones hold lotuses by their stalks and the front right and left hands carry a *cakra* and the miniature figure of Gadādevī. In another sculpture of the same period and of the same region, he has been endowed with four hands, the rear ones holding one leg of each of the divine couple he is carrying on his back and the normal pair being in the *añjali-* or *namaskāra-mudrā*. While the bird-man is yet plastically unknown in his eight-handed form, a six-armed image of the god with a *cakra*, *chatra*, *kamaṇḍalu* and *namaskāra-mudrā* in different hands is reported from Kathmandu, Nepal; it belongs to the seventeenth century. Finally, mention may be made of a sculpture in the Mathura Museum where Garuḍa has been depicted as a standing human being with just a suggestion of tiny wings behind [[P146]] his back, holding a small snake in his hand; this example illustrates, among others, the gradual tendency towards anthropomorphising the bird-man attendant of Viṣṇu.

The 22nd Chapter of the *Hayaśīrṣa Pañcarātra* delineates the [[vyūha|vyūha]][^146_asterisk] (emanatory) aspect of the primeval god Vāsudeva. According to the Pañcarātra philosophy, Viṣṇu expresses himself in four vyūhas (*caturvyūha*), known as Vāsudeva, Saṁkarṣaṇa, Pradyumna and Aniruddha. In course of time, the number of vyūhas rose up to twenty-four (*caturviṁśati-vyūha*). The Pañcarātra literature enjoins that the Supreme lord Vāsudeva creates Saṁkarṣaṇa, Saṁkarṣaṇa in his turn Pradyumna and the latter in his turn Aniruddha; from each of them descends three [[sub- vyūhas|sub-vyūhas]] (*vyūhāntaras*) and to these twelve sub-vyūhas is added again a set of twelve forms, called in some texts Vidyeśvaras (see tables B and C). The process by which the vyūhas are said to emanate is as follows: from Vāsudeva emanates Keśava, Nārāyaṇa and Mādhava; from Saṁkarṣaṇa Govinda, Viṣṇu and Madhusūdana; from Pradyumna Trivikrama, Vāmana and Śrīdhara; and from Aniruddha Hṛṣīkeśa, Padmanābha and Dāmodara; again from para-Vāsudeva appears another Vāsudeva (vyūha-Vāsudeva), from the latter Puruṣottama and from him Janārdana; from Saṁkarṣaṇa another Saṁkarṣaṇa, from him Adhokṣaja, and from the latter Upendra; from Pradyumna another Pradyumna, from him Nṛsiṁha and from the latter Hari; and then from Aniruddha a second Aniruddha, from him Acyuta and from the latter Śrī-Kṛṣṇa. Arithmetically, the permutations of four attributes of Viṣṇu admit of his twenty-four forms. What is important to take note of is that whether in the case of caturmūrti or in that of [[caturvimsati- mūrti|caturviṁśatimūrti]], the principle of [[mono- theism|monotheism]] was never lost sight of. This is also illustrated by the icons of both the groups, i.e., caturmūrtis and caturviṁśati-mūrtis.

The *Hayaśīrṣa* agrees with other Pañcarātra texts and the Purāṇas in explaining the vyūha concept, which is, however, elaborately adumbrated *inter alia*, by the *Viṣṇudharmottara* (III. 85). Our text, however, [[P147]] confines its description only to twelve forms of the god, viz., Keśava, Nārāyaṇa, Mādhava, Govinda, Viṣṇu, Madhusūdana, Vāmana, Trivikrama, Śrīdhara, Hṛṣīkeśa, Padmanābha, and Dāmodara (see Table A).[^147_double_asterisk] Evidently, the present chapter of the *Hayaśīrṣa* was composed before the emergence of the group of caturviṁśati-mūrtis which are described in the *Rūpamaṇḍana, Padma-purāṇa,* Agni-*purāṇa* etc. It says that of these twelve forms Śrīdhara is accompanied by Lakṣmī to his left and each of the last three (viz., Hṛṣīkeśa, Padmanābha and Dāmodara) is flanked by Lakṣmī and Sarasvatī.

[^146_asterisk]: \* Vyūha is one of the five aspects of the Supreme lord, other aspects being para (highest), vibhava (incarnatory), antaryāmī (in-dwelling) and arcā (embodiment). In the antaryāmī aspect the god resides in the hearts of all and in the arcā aspect he manifests himself in the visible or vigraha (iconic) form. Icons described as dhruvaberas ('immovable images') in the Vaikhānasa-āgama appear to have symbolized the para aspect. The vibhava or avatāra forms will be dealt with in the next chapter.
[^147_double_asterisk]: \*\* The Viṣṇudharmottara, for example, refers to the eight hands of the god instead of the usual four (III. 85).

Plastically, the caturvyūhas are combined into one iconic type. The composite image, known as Vaikuṇṭha, shows the lord with four faces and four hands (and rarely more than four arms). Such images mostly hail from Kashmir, the region where the vyūha aspect of Viṣṇu seems to have earned popularity (cf. *caturātmā Keśava* as mentioned by the Kashmirian author Kalhaṇa in his *Rājataraṅgiṇī*). In these icons, the central face is human, side faces on the right and left are of a lion and a boar, and the back face is of an ugly demon; usually the deity is seen holding a lotus and a conch-shell in the front right and left hands respectively and placing his back hands on the personified weapons like Cakrapuruṣa and Gadādevī. While the Mārtaṇḍa temple specimens being relievo representations are three-faced, statues coming from Avantipura, which are in the round, are four-faced. An image from the Mārtaṇḍa temple shows the god with eight hands, most of the hands being, however, broken. An advancement on the usual iconographic form of the caturmūrti, as illustrated by these Kashmir images, can be seen in the increase of the number of hands and sometimes of heads as well. An 11th-century image, now in the Ajmer Museum, showing Viṣṇu with as many as 14 hands, of which five only survive, is a representative example; another interesting feature of this sculpture lies in the presence of Garuḍa, who is seen respectfully carrying his master. The Garuḍāsana caturmūrti (described as 'Vaikuṇṭheśvara' in a contemporary inscription) enshrined in the main sanctum of Lakṣmaṇa temple at Khajuraho is characterised by eight figures (another unusual device) carved on either side of the outer aura of the main image; probably this cluster of figures represent eight of the Keśavādimūrtis and the main image symbolize the four primary ones, viz., Vāsudeva, Saṁkarṣaṇa, Pradyumna and Aniruddha.

The twenty-four forms of the god, as represented in art, are all standing figures, possessing four arms and wearing the *kirīṭamukuṭa* and other usual ornaments; the difference between each of them lies only in the order of the attributes—śaṅkha, cakra, gadā and padma—held by the four hands of the deity; the order of these objects is in passing, as in a circle, from the upper right hand to the upper left hand, thence to the lower left hand and lastly to the lower right hand. Thus the image of the deity showing *śaṅkha, cakra, gadā* and *padma* in the hands in the order mentioned above, beginning from the upper right and ending with the lower right hand, represents the form styled 'Keśava'. The twelve forms of Viṣṇu beginning with Keśava and the order in which the emblems are held in the four hands of the deity, as described in our text, are given in the Tables appended. The *Hayaśīrṣa* list may be compared with those of the *Rūpamaṇḍana* and the *Padma-purāṇa*. It may be noted here that in our text the disposition of attributes in the hands of Keśava (no. 1) is identical with that of Dāmodara (no. 12), and arrangement of the said emblems in the hands of Nārāyaṇa (no. 2) is exactly similar to that in the hands of Padmanābha (no. 11). Similar errors are also noticeable in the *Padma-purāṇa*. In fact, the *Rūpamaṇḍana* list seems to be more correct than those of many other texts.

Barring the Hoysala temples of Mysore of the 12th–13th century, which depict most of these twenty-four forms of the god on their walls, they are not found together forming a single group in early Indian repertory. A 17th-century Nepalese painting, now in the Prince of Wales Museum, Bombay, bears, however, representations of all of them. Stray images of one or other forms have, however, been discovered throughout the country and are preserved in different museums. Among them, Trivikrama is commonly found in Northern India. Generally, caturviṁśatimūrtis are of the *sthānaka* order, but *āsana* examples, though rare, are also not unknown. Thus the Bangiya Sahitya Parisat Museum, Calcutta, possesses a unique bronze image of Śrīdhara (Hṛṣīkeśa according to the *Padma-purāṇa*) showing the deity with *padma*, *cakra,* gadā and *śaṅkha* in the lower right, upper right, upper left and lower left hands of the deity respectively; it is also interesting to note here that with the exception of the first, all the other emblems are placed on full-blown lotuses, springing from stalks held by the god. And further, this statue is in agreement with the description of the *Hayaśīrṣa*, according to which, Śrīdhara may be [[alternativey|alternatively]] depicted as seated. [[P148]]

In the 23rd Chapter iconographic features of ten[^151_ten] Avatāras (incarnatory forms) of Viṣṇu have been delineated. The description of these Avatāras is practically identical with that of other texts. The *Hayaśīrṣa* describes the Avatāras as follows: In the Matsya and Kūrma forms the god comes out of the mouths of a fish and a tortoise respectively; in each case he is visible up to his ankle (that is, his feet are not visible). The Varāha (boar) incarnation portrays him as holding a conch in his left hand and a gadā and a *cakra* in the right hands; he bears Pṛthvī, the Earth-goddess, on his arm (i.e., elbow) and his feet are held by Śeṣa-nāga. In his Narasiṁha (man-lion) form, the god strikes the bosom of the great Asura (named Hiraṇyakaśipu) with the normal pair by felling him on his knees; he is terrific in appearance on account of his sharp teeth, protruding fangs, rolling eyes, shrunken eye-brows and big face. The fifth form of Viṣṇu has two aspects, Vāmana and Trivikrama (in the latter one of his hands and one of his legs are raised upwards, the other leg being planted on earth), while in the former he is up to the height of Trivikrama. As regards Jamadagni (that is Paraśurāma), the exterminator of all Kṣatriyas, he may be endowed with two or four hands; when two-armed, his characteristic emblem *paraśu* is held in one of his two hands, and when four-handed, he carries a paraśu and a *khaḍga* in his right hands and a dhanu and a *kheṭaka* in the left. Image of Dāśarathi Rāma is of the *saptatāla* variety in which the god may be represented with two as well as four hands; if two-armed, he is to hold his usual cognisances, viz., a bow and an arrow, and if four-handed, he is to carry additional emblems of *śaṅkha* and *cakra* in his upper left and right hands respectively. Baladeva or Balarāma carries *muṣala* and *cakra* in the upper and lower right hands and gadā and *kṛpāṇa* (alternatively *śaṅkha* and *cakra*) in the remaining hands. In his Buddha form, Viṣṇu is seated absorbed in meditation with characteristic features like long ears, monk's dress, lotus eyes and lotus-face and inward look. The last form styled Kalki, may show him with four or two hands; if four-armed, he will be carrying *dhanu,* śaṅkha*, cakra* and *khaḍga* and a quiver of arrows at the back; and if he has two hands, he will be holding a *khaḍga* in one of [[it|them]]; in both forms he is to be seen riding on a horse.

In Northern India the group, carved on stone slabs, is placed in [[dif- ferent|different]] parts of the Vaiṣṇava shrines as decorative reliefs. Daśāvatāras also appear in the background of the Viṣṇu sculptures of the mediaeval period. Besides, they are figured on one side of square slabs of stone or metal (the other side bears the effigy of the lord); designated as Viṣṇupaṭṭas, they mostly hail from Bengal.

Separate representations of the Matsya, Kūrma, Paraśurāma and Rāma Avatāras are rare, and the one of Kalki has not been met with as yet. A couple of images of Matsya- and Kūrma- Avatāras, almost exactly agreeing with the *[[Hayasîrṣa|Hayaśīrṣa]]* descriptions, have come to light. The Matsya image, hailing from Vajrayoginī (district Dacca, Bangladesh), shows the four-armed god issuing from the mouth of the fish enthroned on a lotus, while the Kūrma instance (now in the Indian Museum) portrays the deity coming out of the mouth of the tortoise; both these sculptures belong to the 10th–11th century A.D. Mention may also be made of a Kūrmāvatāra icon of about the same period (on display in the Bharat Kala Bhavan, Benares) which depicts a tiny *four*-armed figure of Viṣṇu on the neck of the tortoise, suggesting his emanation from the neck of the animal, as it were. Of the numerous Varāha images which generally tally with the description of our text, mention may be made of the one found at Rajim (M.P.), which shows Śeṣanāga holding Varāha's two feet on the two palms of his hands instead of on his hood. The next Avatāra, as described in the *[[Hayaŝirṣa|Hayaśīrṣa]]*, agrees in form and features with what is designated as Sthauṇa-Narasiṁha in different texts. Several sculptures from Garhwa (U.P.), Paikore (West Bengal) and other places illustrate the killing of Hiraṇyakaśipu by the god in the manner as delineated above. The Vāmana form of Viṣṇu is invariably two-armed, holding an umbrella and a staff, and that of Trivikrama-Virāṭarūpa may be four- or eight-armed, carrying different emblems, with right or left foot firmly planted, the other leg thrown upwards as if to attack the heavens; the Mahābalipuram, the Bādāmi and the Ellora reliefs are some of the early celebrated illustrations. In the Daśāvatāra slabs Paraśurāma invariably appears with two hands, holding a *paraśu* in one of them; his four-handed variety as described by the *[[Hayasırṣa|Hayaśīrṣa]]* and the *Agni-purāṇa* is illustrated by examples from Ranihati (Bangladesh) and Chamba; in the first, the deity is shown [[P153]] with a śaṅkha, a *cakra* and a *gadā* in addition to the *paraśu,* while in the second (of about the 14th century) he holds a *dhanu* in place of a *gadā*; thus both these rare statues of Paraśurāma, though four-armed, do not exactly answer to the *[[Hayasîrṣa|Hayaśīrṣa]]* description, and also to that of the *Agni-purāṇa* which prescribes a bow, an arrow and a sword apart from the *paraśu* for the god. Similarly, in actual representations Rāma is invariably two-armed, though our text alternatively speaks of his four-handed variety. Early images of Balarāma show him as two-armed with the attributes of *hala* and *muṣala,* the earliest representation being discernible on a coin of the Indo-Greek ruler Agathocles (2nd century B.C.), unearthed at Ai-Khanum (Northern Afghanistan). Four-armed specimens of the deity, as referred to by our text, are comparatively rare and belong to a late date; thus the image, now in the V.R.S. Museum, Rajshahi (Bangladesh) shows the deity with *muṣala*, *hala* and *[[pănapātra|pānapātra]]* (wine cup) in his three hands, the other hand being in the *kaṭihasta* pose, and thus does not fulfill the requirements of the *Hayaśīrṣa*. The [[text|next]] Avatāra is Kṛṣṇa (i.e., Kṛṣṇa-Vāsudeva), the earliest plastic representation of whom is met with on the other side of the above-noted coin of Agathocles; in it the lord holds a *cakra,* presumably his cognisance *Sudarśana cakra*. In the Daśāvatāra reliefs of subsequent days, however, Kṛṣṇa's place was taken by Buddha, evidently because Kṛṣṇa was then looked upon as the God himself, in other words, the ever-active god-head (*Kṛṣṇas*-*tu Bhagavān svayam*). In South Indian repertoire, however, Kṛṣṇa was still appearing as an Avatāra. Buddha, where represented, is almost invariably seen standing, with his right hand disposed in the *abhaya-mudrā,* and thus is not in agreement with the *Hayaśīrṣa* description. The two-armed variety of Kalki with a *khaḍga* is recognised in the Daśāvatāra slabs, and his four-armed variety, as enjoined in our text as well as in the *Agni-purāṇa*, has not yet been met with.

The 24th Chapter of the *Hayaśīrṣa Pañcarātra* speaks of the *nava-vyūhas* of Viṣṇu. The concept of nine vyūhas, including Brahmā and Aniruddha, is unusual and interesting. It first refers to the Ādimūrti of Vāsudeva; according to it, when four-handed, the Ādimūrti has to display *gadā* and *cakra* in the upper right and left hands and śaṅkha and *varada* in the normal pair. It then describes a second Vāsudeva who is two-armed and exhibits *śaṅkha* and *varada-mudrā* in his right and left hands. Baladeva carries ploughshare, pestle, mace and lotus; [[P154]] alternatively, he may show a conch-shell, a discus, a mace and *vara.* Pradyumna holds *cakra* and *śaṅkha* in right hands and *dhanu* and *gadā*[^154_O] in the left; if he is accompanied by his wives, Rati and Prīti, he will carry *khaḍga* and *carma* in his hands; Pradyumna may be two-armed as well, exhibiting śara in one of his hands. The cognisances of Aniruddha are *gadā, padma, śaṅkha* and *cakra* of which he holds the first two in his right pair and the third and fourth in the left hands. The other forms of the deity described by our text include Nārāyaṇa, Brahmā, Narasiṁha and Varāha and the vyūha is thus made up of his nine forms.

Nārāyaṇa has been described as identical in respect of iconography with his namesake of the caturviṁśati-vyūha group (Chapter 22). The inclusion of Brahmā in the present group is due either to a misconception or to a deliberate attempt to identify Brahmā with Viṣṇu. However, iconographically, the form described here is typically that of Brahmā: four-faced and four-armed, he has a prominent belly, matted hair and long beard; he carries his usual attributes, *akṣasūtra* and *sruva* in his right hands and *kuṇḍikā* and *ajyasthālī* in the left and is accompanied by his consorts, Sarasvatī and Sāvitrī to his left and right sides respectively. The four-armed Narasiṁha will have to be depicted as killing the great demon, Hiraṇyakaśipu; he is to display śaṅkha and *cakra* in his remaining hands. The last form is Varāha, in which the god may be either four-armed or eight-handed; his attributes are not mentioned, but he has been described, as usual, as holding the earth-goddess with his left arm and placing one of his legs on the coils of Ādiśeṣa. Apart from Brahmā, the two avatāra forms of Viṣṇu, namely Narasiṁha and Varāha have been included in the present vyūha and this seems to be interesting. For plastic representations of Vāsudeva, Pradyumna, Aniruddha and Nārāyaṇa, see Chapter 22 and for Baladeva or Balarāma, Narasiṁha and Varāha, see Chapter 23. As regards images of Brahmā, more or less tallying with the present description, it may be pointed out that they have come from different parts of India, as for instance, from Mirpur Khas (Sind, Pakistan) and Halebid (Karṇāṭaka); in the latter specimen, he is flanked by Sāvitrī and Sarasvatī.

Chapter 25 discusses seven forms of Viṣṇu, viz., Trailokyamohana, Viśvarūpa, Jalaśāyin, Hari-Saṁkara, Varāha, Nṛsiṁha and Aśvavaktra [[P155]] (i.e., Hayagrīva). Trailokyamohana, as the text enjoins, is young in appearance and is eight-armed; in this form the god holds *cakra*, *khaḍga, muṣala* and *aṅkuśa* in his right hands and śaṅkha*, dhanu, gadā* and *pāśa* in the left hands; he rides on the back of Garuḍa and is accompanied by Lakṣmī and Sarasvatī. Endowed with twenty hands, Viśvarūpa-Viṣṇu carries, *cakra*, *khaḍga*, *muṣala*, *aṅkuśa*, *paṭṭiśa*, mudgara, *pāśa*, śakti, *śūla* and *śara* in the right hands and *śaṅkha,* śārṅga, pāśa, *tomara, lāṅgala, paraśu*, *churikā*, daṇḍa*, carma* and *prakṣepana* in the left hands; to his right is four-faced Brahmā and to his left is Śiva; he is worshipped by sages on his all sides and those who move in night are in the act of making an escape. In the next form styled [[Jalakayin|Jalaśāyin]], the god is to be shown as lying in the midst of waters, resting on Śeṣanāga, his serpent-couch and his face is to be oriented towards left; Lakṣmī will be shampooing his legs and Vimalādevī, another consort, will also be nursing him; on the lotus sprung from his navel Brahmā is seen seated; the god possesses four hands and is adorned with all ornaments. The composite form called Hari-Saṁkara, combines the halves of Hari (i.e., Viṣṇu) and Saṁkara (i.e., Śiva), the left half being of Viṣṇu, the right of Śiva; while the left half displays the characteristics of Viṣṇu such as gadā and *cakra* held in the hands, *brahmasūtra*, *kuṇḍala* ear-ring and the *kirīṭa* crown, the Śiva-half shows a śūla in one of the hands, apart from other characteristics like a serpent-thread, an ear-ring made of a serpent and matted hair; to the left is Lakṣmī and to the right is Gaurī. Instead of iconography, a few iconometric prescriptions have been given in regard to Varāha. Four-armed Nṛsiṁha carries a *gadā* and a *cakra* in his right [[hånds|hands]] and a manuscript (consisting of four Vedas) and a śaṅkha in the left; his left leg is held by Śeṣanāga and his right leg is planted on the back of a tortoise. The last form Aśvavaktra (horse-faced), already mentioned under the name of 'Hayagrīva' in Chapter 1, is practically identical with it, except the fact that one of his attributes śārṅga has been substituted by a manuscript (i.e., consisting of the Vedas which were rescued by the god in this form from the hands of Madhu and Kaiṭabha); Aśvavaktra may sit or stand on a lotus-seat and is to be accompanied by his two consorts (viz., Lakṣmī and Sarasvatī).[^155_8]

The different forms of Viṣṇu, as described above, demonstrate similarities as well as differences with their descriptions as met with in [[P156]] other texts. Trailokyamohana, for instance, has been described as sixteen-armed in the *Rūpamaṇḍana* and iconographically he is much similar to the four-faced Vaikuṇṭha. While the *Rūpamaṇḍana* follows the *[[Hayaśīrṣa|Hayaśīrṣa]]* in describing Viśvarūpa as twenty-armed, its description of Viśvarūpa as four-faced is absent in our text. There is not much difference between the *Hayaśīrṣa* and the *Viṣṇudharmottara* in regard to Jalaśāyin. Hari-Saṁkara is same as Hari-Hara and its description is practically identical with that found in other texts, such as, the *Matsya-purāṇa* (260, 22–27) and *Devatāmūrti-prakaraṇa* (VI. 85. 41). While no comment on Varāha is necessary, Nṛsiṁha is not known to have been described or plastically represented with a manuscript in his hand so far. The presence of a *pustaka* in one of the hands of Aśvavaktra or Hayagrīva, signifying him as the bestower of knowledge, has been mentioned in several texts.

Omitting Varāha, Nṛsiṁha and Aśvavaktra from our discussion, we may confine our attention to the iconic representation of the remaining four, viz., Trailokyamohana, Viśvarūpa, Jalaśāyin and Hari-Saṁkara, of which images of the last form are more prolific than those of the others. A 17th-century image, to be seen in the courtyard of the Caṅgu-Nārāyaṇa temple, Nepal, showing the five-headed and ten-armed Viṣṇu, riding on his mount Garuḍa in the company of his consort, Lakṣmī, partially agrees with our description about Trailokyamohana. The earliest representation of Viśvarūpa-Viṣṇu may perhaps be recognised in a fragmentary sculpture, now in the collection of the Mathura Museum. Assignable to the 5th century, it is similar to later specimens, such as those found at Kanauj and Nepal, which are, however, more elaborate than itself. The Kanauj sculpture, which belongs to the 8th–9th century, shows the central deity as standing, eight-armed, and as holding a *śaṅkha,* a *cakra,* a *kheṭaka,* etc.; there is a nāga below his feet and from his neck are emerging the heads of a fish, a tortoise, a boar and a lion, representing the four avatāras (see Ch. 23); while the other avatāras are seen separately above these heads, the supreme lord is surrounded by a number of figures including, *inter alia*, eleven Rudras, one of the Aśvinīkumāras, the twelve Ādityas, etc. In the Mathura sculpture, too, several accessory heads and figures are visible, of which the heads of Varāha and Narasiṁha are distinct and the figure of Buddha may be surmisingly discerned. Perhaps the theme of Viśvarūpa, which has been tellingly delineated in the eleventh canto of the *Bhagavadgītā*, finds one of the best expressions in the 8th-century sculpture in the Caṅgu-Nārāyaṇa temple, Nepal; the great god appears in it with ten heads and ten arms, holding various attributes including the usual ones, viz., *śaṅkha*, *cakra, gadā* and *padma*. Another good specimen of the eleventh century, now belonging to the Varendra Research Society Museum, Rajshahi (Bangladesh), shows Viśvarūpa-Viṣṇu with twenty hands holding various attributes and thus the image follows the *Hayaśīrṣa* prescription.[^156_9] The Jalaśāyin or Jalaśāyana form of the god, exemplified by a 12th-century image found at Halebid, closely follows the description of our text; here Lakṣmī is seated near the feet of Viṣṇu, while what appears to be the figure of Bhūdevī (same as Vimalā) is seen seated near the head; the image, however, shows the daśāvatāras, among others, which are not mentioned in the present work, but are enjoined by later treatises like the *Rūpamaṇḍana*. As regards Hari-Hara or Hari-Saṁkara, two heads (now in the Mathura Museum, Nos. 1333 and 1336), of the second century, each showing portions of *jaṭāmukuṭa* and kirīṭa-*mukuṭa* on the right and left sides respectively, tend to show them to be the earliest remnants of images of the composite god. A better specimen of the Gupta period, now an exhibit of the Patna Museum, shows a trident and a rosary in the hands of the Śiva-half; the upper right hand of the Viṣṇu-half is on the head of the *Cakrapuruṣa* and a conch-shell is in the lower right. This agrees in general to the *Hayaśīrṣa* description, except the *gadā* as required by this text. In fact, gadā is rarely seen in any available specimen. It may be noted in conclusion that the famous image of Veṅkaṭeśa in the shrine on the hill at Tirupati (Andhra) is of Hari-Hara, displaying the usual characteristics of Viṣṇu and Śiva, a *śaṅkha* (?), a *cakra*, *nāgavalaya*, etc.

The iconography of the Grahas or Planets has been described in the 26th Chapter of our text, though in a somewhat condensed manner. More details are to be found in the Purāṇas like the Agni, *Matsya* and *Viṣṇudharmottara* and other treatises such as *Devatāmūrti Prakaraṇa* and *Śilparatna*. While many such texts refer to the vāhanas of the [[P157]] Grahas, the *[[Hayasîrșa|Hayaśīrṣa]]* is silent on this issue, except in the case of Āditya.

According to the present text, Āditya or Sun, the first of the Planets, bears a *padma* and a *[[kheṭaka10|kheṭaka]]*[^158_10] in his two hands and rides on a seven-horsed and one-wheeled chariot; he is flanked by his consorts, Saṁjñā and Niṣkṛtā (Niṣkubhā, according to other texts) as well as his usual male attendants, Daṇḍa and Piṅgala (with their emblems, viz., *khaḍga* and *[[kheṭaka11|kheṭaka]]*[^158_11] and pen and ink-pot); the deity has been described as 'the healer of all [[diseases|diseases]] (*sarva-roga*-*[[vināśanam12|vināśanam]]*[^158_12]); in painted representation he is to be shown in long tunic. The next Graha, Soma or Moon, is two-handed and has a tranquil face. Aṅgāraka or Maṅgala and Budha have been just mentioned and Bṛhaspati (Guru) has been described as benign in appearance. Śukra is one-eyed and Sauri or Śani is lame. Rāhu has a terrible face, a decapitated body with the upper portion only and he exhibits the kṛtāñjali *mudrā*. The last Graha is Ketu, half of whose body is serpentine; he has a grey complexion. The Chapter ends with a prescription that all these Grahas are two-armed and each of them displays *abhayamudrā* in his right hand.

The *Agni-purāṇa* (Ch. 51) prescribes the following attributes in the hands of the Planets: *padma* and *khaḍga* of Ravi, *kuṇḍikā* and *japamālā* of Soma, *śakti* and *akṣamālā* of Maṅgala, cāpa and akṣa of Budha, *kuṇḍi* and akṣamālā of Bṛhaspati and Śukra, kiṅkiṇī and sūtra of Śani, *ardhacandra* of Rāhu, and *khaḍga* and dīpa of Ketu. The *Matsya-* and *Viṣṇudharmottara-purāṇas* and the *Aparājita-pṛcchā* and *Śilparatna* assign chariots and/or animal-mounts to them. According to the *Viṣṇudharmottara* (III. 130), Ravi has a chariot with seven horses, Soma a chariot with ten horses, Maṅgala, Budha and Bṛhaspati each a golden chariot with eight horses, Śukra a silver chariot with ten horses, Śani an iron chariot with eight serpents, Rāhu a silver chariot with eight horses and Ketu a chariot with ten horses. The *Matsya Purāṇa* varies in certain respects. It does not refer to any chariot, but assigns one or many animals to each of the Planets. Thus it alludes to seven horses of Ravi, a white horse of Soma, a ram of Maṅgala, a lion of Budha and a vulture of Śani and Ketu; to Rāhu it gives a throne, while [[P158]] nothing to Bṛhaspati and Śukra. The *Hayaśīrṣa* and many other texts describe the Planets as two-armed, but in some works a few of the Grahas have additional hands (e.g., all the Grahas, except Ravi, Soma and Ketu, are described as four-armed in the *Śilparatna*).

The Planets concerned are usually seen depicted on the lintels or architraves above the doorways of the temples. A fragmentary panel from Sarnath, now in the Indian Museum (exhibit no. 1536), is perhaps the earliest representation of these Grahas. Assignable to the late Gupta period, it now contains the figures of Bṛhaspati, Śukra, Śani and Rāhu, all being two-armed; the first three of them are seen gracefully standing, each having a halo behind his head and an *akṣamālā* in his right hand; the left hands of Bṛhaspati and Śukra carry a water-vessel, while that of Śani is broken; Rāhu is awe-inspiring and has been shown only up to the breast, and his hands are disposed in the *tarpaṇa* or *añjali-mudrā*; the relief ends with Rāhu and hence it is presumable that Ketu lately appeared in the group, at least iconoplastically. That the [[re- presentation|representation]] of Navagrahas in art is later than that of the Aṣṭagrahas, seems to be borne out by the fact that while the reliefs coming from the earlier Śiva temples (e.g., of the Bhauma-Kara period) omit Ketu, those of the Gaṅgā period onwards contain the figures of all the nine Grahas. Another interesting fact is that in some mediaeval examples the group [[repre- sentation|representation]] is preceded by Gaṇapati (e.g., the Kaṅkāndīghī relief, now in the Ashutosh Museum).

In the Navagraha slabs, the Planets are usually shown as standing. A good specimen depicting the Grahas as seated may be seen in the art gallery of the Directorate of Archaeology of West Bengal. And as regards the *vāhanas*, panels depicting the Planets without mounts appear to be earlier than those with *vāhanas*. Examples showing the mounts of the planets are not rare and the Bangiya Sahitya Parisat Museum and the art gallery of the Directorate of Archaeology of West Bengal contain some such slabs, though they along with others do not exactly conform to the textual prescription, so far as the *vāhanas* of the Planets are concerned. Mention may be made of an extremely interesting sculpture, a twelve-spoked wheel resting on its side on a lotus pedestal, now in the Asutosh Museum; inside the broad rim of this Navagraha Cakra are carved the figures of nine Planets and in its centre is a three-faced and a four-handed figure seated in *padmāsana* and displaying the *dhyānamudrā* in the normal pair and holding indistinct objects in the back hands; this central figure most probably represents Brahmā. The 27th Chapter deals with the iconography of Seven Mothers, viz., Brahmāṇī, Rudrāṇī (Māheśvarī of other lists), Kaumārī, Vaiṣṇavī, Vārāhī, Māhendrī (Indrāṇī or Aindrī of other lists) and Cāmuṇḍā, as well as their male companions Vīrabhadra and Vināyaka. Of them four are two-armed, the rest four-handed. The description of these Mothers, as found in the *Hayaśīrṣa Pañcarātra,* does not substantially differ from that of other texts. Elsewhere (the *Bṛhatsaṁhitā*, Ch. 57 and *Mānasāra*, Ch. 54) it is stated that the Mātṛkās should have the same attributes and [[charac- teristic|characteristic]] features as their male counterparts, viz., Brahmā, Rudra, Viṣṇu, etc.

The *Hayaśīrṣa* describes the Mothers thus: 
1. **Brahmāṇī** is four-faced and displays *vara* in the right hand and a *kamaṇḍalu* in the left; she is bedecked with all sorts of ornaments and a *yogapaṭṭa* goes round her legs and waist and she is riding astride on a swan;
2. **Rudrāṇī** carries *śūla* and *śara* in her right hands and *vajra* and *kārmuka* in the left; she is three-eyed and rides on a bull;
3. **Kaumārī** rides on a peacock and holds *śakti* in one of her hands;
4. **Vaiṣṇavī** is four-armed, adorned with all ornaments, and carries a *śaṅkha*, a *cakra* and a *gadā* in three of her hands (the other attribute is not mentioned), and *pakṣirāja,* i.e., Garuḍa is her mount;
5. **Vārāhī**, with the face of a boar and a flabby belly rides on a buffalo; her emblems include *daṇḍa*, *śaṅkha,* cakra and gadā;
6. **Māhendrī** is two-armed, holding a *vajra* in one of her hands; she is youthful in appearance, has several eyes and rides on an elephant;
7. **Cāmuṇḍā** is emaciated, practically without any flesh, has a haggard look and dishevelled hair; she wears elephant-skin and serpent-thread, carries kapāla and *paṭṭiśa* in left hands and *śūla* and *kartrī* in the right; she rides on a human corpse and has a terrible appearance;
8. **Vīrabhadra**, who precedes Brahmāṇī, is endowed with three eyes and his matted hair is marked by a crescent; he rides on a bull and carries a [[viņā|vīṇā]];
9. **Vināyaka** or Gaṇeśa is [[bot-bellied|pot-bellied]] and three-eyed and carries his own broken tusk and a *paraśu* in right hands and a pot full of sweetmeat and a lotus in the left pair; he is accompanied by the cat-eyed Sumukha.

The present chapter of our text also contains a description of Skanda, according to which this deity, boyish in appearance, carries a [[ukkuta|kukkuṭa]] and *śakti* in the left and right hands respectively; alternatively, Skanda can be six- or twelve-armed.

The 28th Chapter deals with the iconography of Lokeśas, i.e., the Dikpālas or the 'guardians of the quarters' (or the worlds), the number whereof is eight.[^163_asterisk] They are to be represented on a lotus-seat or pedestal (seated or standing), if they are not shown on their respective mounts.

[^163_asterisk]: \* Chapter 34, while describing the rituals connected with Dikpālas, refers to their characteristic attributes. In it, Agni is, however, endowed with a *śakti*. This chapter also includes Brahmā and Ananta in the list of Dikpālas as the guardian-deities of the zenith and nadir respectively.

**Indra**, the guardian of the east, holds *vajra* in one of his hands and rides on his elephant mount, called Airāvata; he is bedecked with all kinds of ornaments and is endowed with many eyes; he is best among the kings.

**Agni**, the guardian of the south-eastern quarter, is seated on a ram with a *yogapaṭṭa* going round his knees; he carries an *akṣasūtra* in his right hand and a *kuṇḍikā* in his left; flames are seen round his body; he wears a *brahmasūtra*, has a matted hair and beard and his complexion is deep red.

**Yama** or Vaivasvata, the lord of the south, holds a *pāśa* and a *daṇḍa* in his hands and has buffalo as his mount; he is flabby, wears costly ornaments and is accompanied by uncouth attendants; two awe-inspiring figures are to be seen on his two sides.

**Nirṛti**, the guardian of the south-western quarter, is not mentioned by name. The lord of bears, cows and *rakshasas,* the deity holds a *khaḍga* in [[one|one of]] his hands. He is flabby and dark-complexioned and wears ornaments. The text does not mention his *vāhana.*

**Varuṇa**, the lord of the west, rides on a *makara* and carries a *pāśa* in one of his hands. He wears *kuṇḍalas* in his ears.

**Vāyu**, the guardian of the north-west, is bedecked with all sorts of valuable ornaments; he rides on an antelope and carries a banner; he is a benevolent deity.

**Kubera** or Yakṣeśa, the guardian of the northern quarter, [[carries ‹ gadā|carries a gadā]] and a *śaṅkha*; he wears a *mukuṭa* and ornaments like *hāra* and *keyūra,* apart from several other jewels; he is flabby in appearance.

**Īśāna**, the guardian of the north-eastern quarter, rides on a bull. Unlike the previous Lokeśas, he is four-handed, with *kapāla* and *nāgapāśa* in the left pair and *śūla* and *vara* in the right pair; his complexion is as white as a *śaṅkha* and a *kunda* flower.

The descriptions of the Lokeśas, given above, are more or less similar to those of other texts. Thus in respect of Agni, we have identical expressions like *lamba-kūrcha*-*dhara* and *jvālāvitāna* in the *Matsya-purāṇa* (Ch. 261).

Regarding the attributes and *vāhanas* of the deities, the *Hayaśīrṣa* does not differ much from other texts, though it does not say anything about the mounts of Nirṛti and Kubera. Some of the noteworthy differences are as follows: the *Agni-purāṇa* (Ch. 51) refers to *śakti* as an emblem of Agni which does not find mention in our text; according to the *Hayaśīrṣa*, ram (*avi*) is the mount of Agni, but the *Agni-purāṇa* (Ch. 51) assigns that animal to Kubera and like the *Matsya-purāṇa* [[P164]] it refers to a goat as the *vāhana* of Agni; while the *Hayaśīrṣa* and the *Agni-purāṇa* mention buffalo as the mount of Yama, the *Matsya-purāṇa* (Ch. 261) substitutes the animal by a human corpse; Kubera, according to the latter text, carries eight *nidhis* (jewels) in one of his hands (*śaṅkha* being however one of these *nidhis*), a description of which is not found in the *Hayaśīrṣa*; in addition it describes the deity as pot-bellied. The *Bṛhatsaṁhitā* and some other texts endow Indra with the third eye placed horizontally on his forehead, but [[out|our]] text along with many others describes the deity as having several or thousand eyes. Again, according to the *Agni-purāṇa*, all the lokapālas should be two-armed (*dvivāhavo lokapālā*), but the *Hayaśīrṣa* endows four hands to Īśāna.

A few of these Lokeśas or Dikpālas are recognized in the basement sculptures at Paharpur (Rajshahi, Bangladesh): Indra standing facing east, before his *vāhana*, exhibits *vara* in his right hand and an indistinct object (citrus) in the left and has the third eye (as enjoined by the *Bṛhatsaṁhitā*) placed horizontally on the forehead. Agni, also standing, carries an *akṣasūtra* and a *kuṇḍikā* in his hands and is characterised by flames surrounding his body; his *vāhana* is, however, absent. Yama, facing south, stands erect carrying a *pāśa* passing over his head with the ends hanging down; he is flanked by two figures, a male and a female, probably identical with his attendants Citragupta and Dhūmrorṇā, but here also the mount of the deity is absent.

Some very good examples of the figures of Lokeśas, met with in several temples of Bhuvanesvara, answer to our descriptions. At Bhuvanesvara, the Lokeśas first appear on the *jagamohana* of the Paraśurāmeśvara temple (c. A.D. 650), though not in their respective positions; all of them are portrayed as seated. The Rajrani temple (c. tenth century), among others, contains some beautiful sculptures of the Lokeśas, the most noteworthy being that of Varuṇa. Here on the corner projections of the lower *jāṅgha* ('shin') these Lokeśas stand on lotuses, with their mounts below, carrying their characteristic attributes (where visible or surviving). Indra holds a *vajra* and an *aṅkuśa,* Yama a *daṇḍa* and a *pāśa,* Nirṛti a *khaḍga* and a severed head,[^164_17] Varuṇa a *pāśa* and *varada*-*mudrā* and Vāyu a *dhvaja*; the hands of Agni are broken, Kubera is seen above seven jars of *nidhi* and Īśāna is depicted by the side of an emaciated figure; the figure of Nirṛti answers to some texts according to which the deity rides on a man. On the walls of the sanctum of the Brahmeśvara temple (c. A.D. 1060) the Lokeśas are seen seated on their respective mounts (except Kubera who is without any *vāhana*) and carrying their characteristic emblems; here the well-preserved figure of Agni shows the deity with an *akṣamālā* and a *kuṇḍikā* in his hands; Īsāna like other Lokeśas is two-handed holding a *triśūla* and a *gadā* and thus does not fully answer to the *Hayaśīrṣa* [[descrip- tion|description]] of the four-armed Īśāna with *kapāla, nāgapāśa, śūla* and *varada-mudrā* as his attributes.

In discussing the iconography of Rudra, the 29th Chapter of the *Hayaśīrṣa Pañcarātra* refers to the five specific forms of the god: Rudra, Hara, Ardhanarīśvara, Umeśa and Bhairava. It enjoins that Rudra should sit on a lotus-seat, wear tiger-skin and an *upavīta* made of [[P165]] serpent, bear a crescent moon on his matted hair and hold a *kapāla* and a *kuṇḍikā* in his left hands and a *triśūla* and a *galantikā* in his right hands; he is flanked by Kūṣmāṇḍa and Bhṛṅgī on his left and right respectively. Hara, the next form, is said to have exhibited *triśūla* and *vara* in his left and right hands respectively. The third form, Ardhanarīśvara, combines Śiva and Pārvatī in one body, the right and left halves representing the god and his consort respectively. The relevant passages of the chapter aver that while the Śiva-half exhibits a crescent moon on the matted hair, *ūrdhvalinga, upavīta,* tiger-skin, serpent-girdle, ear-ring, and *akṣasūtra* and *triśūla* as attributes held in hands, the female or the Pārvatī-half shows the deity as carrying *kuṇḍikā* and *kamala* in her hands and wearing ornaments and jewelleries like *bālikā*, *keyūra*, *kaṅkaṇa*, *hāra*, *mekhalā*, etc. and as having her hair arranged in a tuft. In the next form, Umeśa, the three-eyed and two-armed god is seated in *ardhachandrāsana* by the side of Umā, his left hand going round the back of his consort and the right bearing an *indīvara*; Umā, who is seated on his left thigh in his close embrace, places her right hand on the shoulder of Śiva and holds an *ādarśa* in her left hand. The last form, Bhairava, signifies the terrific aspect of the god; according to our text, Bhairava is three-eyed and eighteen-handed, carrying weapons and attributes like *triśūla, dhanu, kṛpāṇa, khaṭvāṅga, śakti,* kaṅkāla, pāśa, *śara, kheṭaka, mudgara, vara, abhaya, gadā, paraśu, kapāla* and *muṇḍa*; he is endowed with a fierce look, a yawning mouth, protruding fangs, sharp teeth, and a crescent moon on his matted hair; he wears a tiger-skin, serpent-thread and a garland of skulls, and roams about in cremation grounds and forests in the company of goblins, [[demonaic|demoniac]] creatures and female attendants; Bhairava, the text states, may be one- or five-faced and pierces a great demon by his *triśūla.*

The different forms of Rudra or Rudra-Śiva, described above, illustrating both the benign and terrific aspects of the god, do not vary much from the descriptions met with elsewhere. Minor variations occur in respect of attributes or in the number of hands of Śiva or Pārvatī. Thus elsewhere (e.g., in the *Matsya-purāṇa*) Pārvatī in the Ardhanarīśvara form alternatively holds a *darpaṇa* and Hara in the Umeśa aspect carries *śūla* instead of an *indīvara*. The second form, Hara, may be illustrated by a bronze image discovered at Manir Tat (the district of 24 Parganas, West Bengal) and a stone sculpture at Paharpur (Rajshahi district, Bangladesh); in the former, the deity with *ūrdhvalinga* and *jaṭā*- [[P166]] *mukuṭa* features, is standing in *samapāda-sthānaka* on a lotus over a rectangular pedestal, placing his left hand on a *triśūla,* as it were, the emblem being placed on the pedestal; his right hand is held downwards; and though the palm has disappeared, it appears from what remains, that it was held in *varada-mudrā;* the Paharpur specimen, practically identical with the previous one, shows the god standing with a slight flexion, exhibiting *varada* and *triśūla* in right and left hands respectively, apart from the features like *ūrdhvalinga* and *jaṭāmukuṭa*; both these images may be assigned to the 7th century A.D.

Images of other forms of the deity, viz., Ardhanarīśvara, Umeśa and Bhairava, so far found, do not exactly correspond to the respective descriptions. While images hailing from South India do not show the composite deity of Ardhanarīśvara with penis erect (e.g., Mahābalipuram, Kumbhakonam, etc.), those of the North Indian repertory are of the *ūrdhvalinga* type. Further, some of the South Indian sculptures represent the god with three instead of four or more hands (e.g., the above-noted Kumbhakonam figure and a similar one from Conjeeveram). The Ellora and Bādāmi reliefs of the four-armed composite deity bear attributes like *paraśu, kamala, darpaṇa*, etc., and he is without the *ūrdhvalinga* feature. The latter feature is almost persistently present in sculptures of Northern and Eastern India from an early time, as illustrated by the early examples coming from Mathura of the 4th–5th century A.D. and a late specimen from Purapara (near Dacca, Bangladesh, now in the Rajshahi Museum) of the 12–13th century A.D. The last-mentioned image may be cited as a relevant example showing the continuity of the *ūrdhvalinga* tradition as recorded in the *Hayaśīrṣa* and similar texts.

Representations of Hara-Gaurī, termed here as Umeśa, are of [[com- mon|common]] occurrence. The deities are seen either standing or seated side by side. Our text, however, describes the seated type only. Hara-Gaurī images, so far discovered, depict the god with more than two hands, usually with four hands and thus they are at variance with the *Hayaśīrṣa* description of the two-armed form; Gaurī is, however, two-handed. Such images in general agree with the textual descriptions of Hara-Gaurī. Thus a relief from Mathura, now in the collection of the local Museum, portrays both Śiva and his consort with a lotus (*indīvara*) in [[P167]] each of their hands; hence it tallies with the *Hayaśīrṣa* description so far as Śiva is concerned, but disagrees with the same when the attribute of Gaurī is found to be a lotus in place of an [[adarsa|ādarśa]]. Another sculpture of the same period discovered from Kosam (U.P.), depicts the divine couple in the *sthānaka* posture, each of them with two hands; the goddess bears an *ādarśa* in her left hand as required by our text, but the god carries a flask in the left hand in place of a lotus. In both these instances the god is ithyphallic, a feature absent in the present textual description. Images of the Orissan school, as exemplified by the Bhuvanesvara specimens, portray the god with four arms. Thus a sculpture on the Megheśvara temple (12th century) shows Hara with *abhaya-mudrā*, an *akṣasūtra*, a *triśūla* in three of his hands and embracing Gaurī with the remaining hand; Pārvatī, as enjoined by our text, is found seated on the lap of his consort, holding a *darpaṇa* in her left hand and throwing her other hand round the neck of her lord. In the *jagamohana* of the same temple may be seen an interesting sculpture where Śiva has been portrayed with several heads (three are only visible) and with six hands; the three right hands exhibit *vara*, *akṣasūtra* and a *paraśu*; of the two surviving left hands one is embracing Gaurī, the other holding a lotus; the goddess is seated on the lap of Hara, her right hand going round the neck of her lord and the left carrying a lotus. Another specimen (no. I/31/289) showing Hara with eight arms (attributes: *triśūla*, *cakra*, *sarpa*, *[[mātulunga|mātuluṅga]]* etc.) is on display in the Ajmer Museum.

The last form of Rudra, known as Bhairava, may be illustrated by several examples, but an eighteen-armed example is not known to us as yet. Most of these representations answer in general to the description of Bhairava found in the *[[Hayaŝirṣa|Hayaśīrṣa]]* and other texts. In illustration, a late mediaeval statue of Bhairava, now in the Asutosh Museum, may be described. In it the ten-armed deity has been shown wearing a garland of skulls, among other things, and carrying weapons like *khaḍga*, *śūla, dhanu, śara*, *kheṭaka,* etc.; he stands on a supine figure and pierces his breast by his *śūla* and is flanked by a plump male and a lean female. Figures of Bhairava with four and eight hands have been recovered from different places of India.

The 30th Chapter is concerned with three forms of [[Gaurī.13|Gaurī.]][^167_18] **Ambikā**, it states, is two-armed and three-eyed and is to ride on a lion; she holds a *triśūla* and a *darpaṇa* in her right and left hands respectively; alternatively, she may be four-armed, exhibiting a *triśūla,* a *galantī,* a [[P168]] *kuṇḍikā* and *varada-mudrā* in her hands and in such form she is to be shown on a lotus pedestal. The next form of the goddess is **Lalitā**; flanked by Gaṇeśa and Kārttikeya, she is to be portrayed as placing one of her left hands on the head of the latter and as carrying an *ādarśa* and a *śrīphala* in two of her remaining hands; she is to be seen on a lotus-seat. In her **Caṇḍikā** form, the great goddess appears as fighting with the buffalo-demon riding on the back of her leonine mount while her right leg is planted on the back of the lion; her left leg is placed on the decapitated head of the buffalo; she is about to kill the demon who is coming out of the neck of the buffalo without least effort, as it were, and is seen binding him by her serpent-noose and thrusting her trident in his body; the wounded demon is biting his lips in anger and vomiting blood; Caṇḍikā carries *śūla*, *khaḍga*, *cakra, śakti* and *iṣu* in her right hands and *nāgapāśa,* carma, *paraśu,* aṅkuśa and *dhanu* in the left.

[^167_18]: 18 Iconography of Gaurī has also been dealt with in the 2nd chapter of the *Lainga Kāṇḍa.*

The three forms of Gaurī, as described above, agree in general with those delineated in other texts. Variations in iconography are of a minor nature. Lalitā may display a *śaṅkha* and a *śalākā* (or a small box containing collyrium for the eyes), in addition to a mirror and a fruit. Similarly, Caṇḍikā, same as Mahiṣāsuramardinī, may be endowed with more than ten hands, and in fact, the number of her hands have been found varying from two to thirty-two.[^168_19] The *Viṣṇudharmottara*, for example, endows her with twenty hands placing the *śūla*, *khaḍga*, *śaṅkha, cakra, bāṇa,* śakti, vajra, *abhaya*, *ḍamaru* and an umbrella in the right ones and *nāgapāśa, kheṭaka*, *paraśu, aṅkuśa*, *dhanu*, *ghaṇṭā*, *dhvaja*, *gadā,* a mirror and the *mudgara*. There is a remarkable similarity in the descriptions of the fight of the goddess with the buffalo-demon as met with in the *Hayaśīrṣa* and the *Viṣṇudharmottara*.

No image of Ambikā and Lalitā, fully agreeing with the *Hayaśīrṣa* descriptions, have been met with so far.[^168_20] In fact, the worship of these [[P170]] [[nilder|milder]] forms of Devī is connected more with the mystic geometrical drawings known as *cakras* and *pīṭhas* than with concrete representations; thus the Śrī-Cakra consisting of several intersecting triangles surrounded by circles and squares is one of such mystic figures allegedly sacred to Lalitā. The reliefs of Ellora and Mahābalipuram are two representative illustrations depicting the fighting postures of Caṇḍikā; in them the goddess is eight-armed. The ten-armed form of the goddess, as described by our text, appears to have become the conventional type in subsequent days. One of the earliest icons of this ten-handed form, belonging to the ninth-tenth century, has been discovered at Dulmi, in the district of Manbhum; the goddess has been portrayed here as slaying the demon with her right and left legs planted on the lion and buffalo respectively; she displays *triśūla*, *kheṭaka*, *ṭaṅka*, *śara* and *khaḍga* in her right hands and *dhanu*, *paraśu, aṅkuśa*, *nāgapāśa* and *sūchīmudrā* in the left hands. Another almost similar image, with the inscription *Śrī-māṣika Caṇḍī* in the 12th-century characters on its pedestal, may now be seen in the Dacca Museum.
